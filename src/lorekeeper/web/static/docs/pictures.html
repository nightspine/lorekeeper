Pictures

Lorekeeper can draw pictures for a book: one for each chapter, more for the moments worth seeing, and a portrait of each main character. The Reader shows a picture only once you have read up to the point it belongs to.

Pictures are off until you ask for them. They need an image provider, which is a Gemini or OpenAI key, and a cloud text model to write the prompts. Claude cannot draw, but it can write the prompts while Gemini or OpenAI draws. Pictures are kept beside the index, never inside it, so an index you share or install stays text only.

What can be drawn

  • Chapter pictures. One picture for the main moment of a chapter. It sits under the chapter's title on the Chapters tab.
  • Moments. A picture for another moment of a chapter, shown at the left of its event on the Timeline.
  • Portraits. One for each of the book's main characters: the people and creatures the story is most about, up to 12 a book. A character counts as main when it is named in at least one in twenty of the book's events, and in at least 3. Small moments do not count. When more than 12 qualify, the 12 named most are drawn. "Everyone with a part" draws a wider cast: every person and creature with a description who is named in two of the book's events, small moments not counted, or who comes up often enough to get a description written from every passage about them. In Dracula that is 32 of the 51 characters with a description. The portrait sits beside "Who they are" in the entry, and a small round one replaces the type mark in the Cast list. When the character's look changes for good, a new portrait is drawn and the old one is kept, so the entry can show every portrait through the book (see On an entry). A lasting wound or scar, a lost eye, a disguise, a body remade or a new way of dressing all count. A death alone does not. A character has at most three portraits per book. You can still draw a portrait of anyone else from their entry in the Reader.
  • Chapter comics. One page of panels of a chapter's moments, made on demand from the Timeline (see Chapter comics).
  • Places and things. These are never drawn ahead of time, but you can draw any of them on demand from its entry.

To plan the portraits of a run, a cheap text call reads every detail and description the index holds about a character in the book. For each one it says whether it tells how they look, and whether it records a lasting change of their look. It only marks items. Nothing it writes goes into a prompt, and an item counts only from its own point in the book. A first portrait is drawn at the first item that describes the look, else at the first description, and a new version at each change, at most three a book. The call works the same way for any book, since it reads the index's own words rather than a list of look words. It is made only for a run. Drawing one portrait from the Reader never waits for it.

How many pictures a chapter gets

Before drawing a chapter, a cheap text call reads the chapter's events and picks the ones that can be drawn: a moment with a place, people or things in view. "Harker finds every door locked" can be drawn. "Harker concludes he is a prisoner" cannot. Small moments are never drawn. That count is the most pictures the chapter can have.

When you draw a whole book, you choose one of three levels for chapter pictures:

  • The main moment of each chapter draws one picture per chapter. It is the chapter's turning point when one can be drawn, else the moment the chapter recap says most about, else the recap itself.
  • Every moment worth drawing draws all the moments the check picked.
  • No chapter pictures draws no chapter pictures and no moments, only portraits. With "Main characters" you get the portraits of the main characters and nothing else. No chapter is checked at this level.

A chapter with no events gets no picture. The Reader's picture sheet makes no such call: for a chapter not checked yet, it offers every moment that is not a small one. In the Reader, the picture sheet for a chapter says how many moments it has so far and how many are drawn, for example "4 moments in this chapter so far, 1 drawn", and you can draw the rest one at a time.

Styles

Nine styles come with Lorekeeper: Digital art, Comic strip, Anime, Realism, Hand-drawn, Oil painting, Epic sci-fi, Watercolour and Ink. Each is one sentence added to every prompt, such as "Black ink drawing, fine cross-hatching, strong contrast, aged paper." for Ink. You can add your own styles too.

Choosing a style

You choose a style from a grid of previews. The grid is the same wherever a style is chosen: the picture sheet, the Pictures group of the build form, the book page's Add pictures dialog and Settings.

Every preview shows the same scene, so you can compare the styles like for like. It is the Mad Tea-Party from Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, a public domain book. A long table is laid under a tree, with a girl in an armchair at one end, a hare and a man in a very tall hat talking, and a dormouse asleep in a teapot. The scene is written in Lorekeeper's own words, with no names and no illustrator, and every preview is drawn from that one description plus the style's sentence. That is how a real prompt is built too.

In the picture sheet and the dialogs, a row shows the chosen style's thumbnail and name with Change. It opens the grid in a sheet titled "Styles". Choosing a card picks the style and closes the sheet. The chosen card has a ring around it. On a phone the grid is three cards across.

Each card with a preview has a button in its corner that shows the preview larger, with the style's sentence under it. A card with no preview shows its sentence instead. A style of your own with a reference picture (see A picture of the style) shows that picture when it has no preview, marked "Your picture". With a preview, the card shows the preview and a small copy of your picture in its corner. The larger view shows both, and says whether your picture is sent with drawings.

In Settings, under Pictures, the grid sits in the page as the Styles section. Choosing a card there makes it the style new pictures start in. Settings always shows the tea party.

Once a book has its own style previews, the grid shows them for that book instead (see Style previews from your book). The tea party stays as the fallback: on the Settings page, for a book with no previews yet, and on the card of any style the book has no preview of.

Style previews from your book

The tea party shows how the styles differ, but not how your book looks in them. So Lorekeeper can draw one scene from your book in every style, and show those pictures on the style cards whenever you choose a style for that book.

When they are drawn. After a book's first build in the web app, a small run draws them on its own. It does so only when Draw style previews for new books is on in Settings, under Pictures (it is on unless you turn it off), when a Gemini or OpenAI key is set up, and when the book has no previews yet. The build's log says it queued them and what they will cost. A rebuild never draws them again, and lorekeeper illustrate never draws them. For a book built before, use the book page (below).

The scene comes from the opening only. You see the style cards before you read and while you read, so the scene is always one moment from the book's first two story chapters. A contents page, a title page or a "Part One" divider does not count as a chapter. By default it is the main moment of Chapter 1, the one a chapter picture would show. Its prompt is written once, by the cheap text model of your cloud provider, from the opening alone. It uses nothing from an earlier book of a series, and no portrait or other picture is sent with it, since the previews compare styles, not faces.

How they are drawn. The same prompt is drawn in every style, once for each image provider with a key, so Gemini and OpenAI each get their own set. Each picture is square, from the provider's default image model at its cheapest quality. A style of your own with a reference picture sends it, as its own preview does. If a provider refuses one style, or fails, that style is skipped and the rest are drawn.

Where they show. In the picture sheet, the build form's Pictures group when you build a book again, and the book page's Add pictures dialog, each card shows the book's preview for the provider chosen there. One quiet line above the grid says "Drawn from a scene in Chapter 1." When the book has previews from both Gemini and OpenAI, a switch above the grid moves between them. If the chosen provider has no previews of the book, the cards show the tea party. A saved style whose description or picture changed after its preview was drawn says "Preview is out of date".

On the book page. A Style previews row says where they stand: "Drawn for Gemini and OpenAI from a scene in Chapter 1.", "Drawing, 4 of 9." or "None yet.".

  • Draw style previews draws them for a book that has none, with the price beside it.
  • Redraw draws every style again from the same scene. Draw the N missing draws only the styles with no preview or an out-of-date one, such as a style you added later.
  • Choose another scene lists the moments of the first two chapters, each with its chapter and what happens, the current one marked "Drawn now", and the price. Choosing one and Draw writes a new prompt and draws every style from it. Its pictures take the place of the old scene's.
  • Remove previews asks first. The book's style cards go back to the tea party.

The run shows in Runs as "Style previews", with "4 of 9 pictures" and its cost. Stopping it keeps what it drew. It never holds up a build, an edit or another pictures run, and only one previews run at a time draws for a book.

What it costs. With Gemini, nine styles at Nano Banana 2.1's cheapest level are 9 times $0.0336, about 30 cents a book. On Dracula the whole run cost $0.30, plus $0.0016 for the prompt. OpenAI's default image model, GPT Image 2.6 Flare, publishes no price per picture, so its previews show "price unknown" before they are drawn. Each style you add adds one picture per provider. A run whose estimate is over $5 is refused, and a run stops if it reaches $5.

Your own styles

The last card of the grid is New style. It opens a form with two fields:

  • Name, up to 40 characters, such as "Linocut".
  • Reference picture, optional: a picture in a style you like (see A picture of the style).
  • Description, up to 300 characters, such as "a linocut print in two colours, bold carved lines". This is the sentence added after every prompt drawn in the style. Describe how it looks, never an artist's name.

Draw a preview draws the same tea-party scene in your style, so it sits beside the built-in ones. Its price is shown beside the button, about 3 cents with Gemini at standard quality. It uses the image provider, model and quality set in Settings, under Pictures, and it needs a Gemini or OpenAI key. If the style is not saved yet, drawing a preview saves it first. If the provider refuses to draw it, the form says so with the provider's reason, and the old preview stays. Save keeps the style. A preview is optional: a style without one shows its sentence on its card.

A picture of the style

A style of your own can carry one picture in the style you want, such as a print, a painting or a page you like. Drop it on the form's Reference picture field, or choose Choose a picture. It must be a PNG, JPEG or WebP file of at most 8 MB, and each side must be 256 to 8192 pixels. The server checks the file itself, not its name. A picture that breaks a rule is refused with a sentence under the field that says which rule. Once chosen, the picture shows as a thumbnail with Replace and Remove. It is sent when you choose Save, and it says "Not saved yet. Save sends it." until then. Removing a saved picture asks first. The built-in styles cannot have a picture.

Describe it for me sits beside the Description when the form has a picture. It sends the picture to a text model that can see pictures, which writes the sentence for you: one or two plain phrases about how the picture is made, such as its medium, line, colours, texture and light. It never names what the picture shows, an artist or a title. If you have already typed a description, it asks "Replace your description?" first. The model is the strong model of the first cloud provider you set up whose strong model can see pictures, else a cheap one that can. The form then says which model wrote it and what it cost, a fraction of a cent. Nothing is stored by describing. Without a cloud model that can see pictures, the form says so and links to Settings, Cloud models.

The description is still what every prompt carries, and it is still required. Read it and change it where it is wrong. The picture adds to the sentence and never replaces it, so a wrong description is not put right by the picture: in a test, a bright comic picture described as "muted sepia" was drawn in sepia, with or without the picture sent.

Send this picture with every drawing in this style is ticked when you first add a picture. While it is on, every picture drawn in the style also gets your picture: in a run, from the picture sheet, on a redraw with the same prompt, and for the style's preview. It goes after the portraits used for faces, as the last picture the image model is given, with a note that says to take only the drawing style from it: its medium, line, colour, texture and light, never its subjects, figures, faces, composition or setting. In a test of 17 pictures, nothing from a reference picture's scene showed up in a drawing. Untick it to keep the picture with the style without sending it. Fix never sends it, since a fix keeps the picture's own look (see Fixing a drawing fault).

Your picture leaves the server. It goes to the image provider, such as Gemini or OpenAI, with each picture drawn in the style, and to the text model when you choose Describe it for me. Choose a picture you are happy to send to them. A picture drawn with it says "Style from your reference picture." under How this was drawn.

Saved styles belong to the server, not to one browser, so everyone who uses it sees the same list. A style you made in the picture sheet or a dialog is chosen at once. One made in Settings does not change your default.

Each style needs its own name. A name is refused when it matches a built-in style or another saved one, ignoring case, spaces and punctuation, so "ink!" counts as Ink and "digital art" as Digital art. "Custom" and "Refs" are kept for the app's own use and cannot be style names, and neither can a name with no letters or digits. A refused name says "There is a style with that name already. Choose another name." under the field.

Changing and deleting a style

In Settings, each of your own styles has Edit and Delete on its card. The built-in styles cannot be changed.

Edit opens the same form. A new name only changes what the style is called. Pictures drawn in it stay in the same set. A new description changes the pictures drawn in the style from then on, in every book. Pictures already drawn stay as they are. Adding, replacing or removing the picture, or switching whether it is sent, changes the pictures drawn from then on in the same way. If the preview was drawn from the old description, or with another picture or none, the card says "Preview is out of date" until you draw a new one.

Delete asks first. A deleted style can no longer be chosen for new pictures. Pictures already drawn in it stay, and the Reader still shows them. Its sets keep drawing as they did, with its words, and with its picture when it was sent, and creating a style with the same name takes them back. If it was your default in Settings, the default goes back to Digital art.

Styles and prompts

Each style is a separate set of pictures. You can draw a book in Ink and again in Watercolour, and the Reader's style menu switches between them.

The style is not part of what the text model writes. A prompt is the model's description, then the style sentence, then a sentence asking for no text in the picture. So changing the style in the picture sheet is instant: the sheet swaps the style sentence and writes nothing again. If you edited the prompt, the old style sentence is replaced where it still stands at the end. If you took it out, the sheet leaves your text alone and says that the style you chose is not in the prompt. Shape, quality, provider and model are instant too.

How a prompt is written

Each picture's prompt is written by one call to a text model. It reads the index and the book as they stand at the picture's point, each part marked with where it comes from, and writes a description: 60 to 150 words for a scene, 50 to 110 for a portrait. For a portrait, a place or a thing it reads:

  • The entry. What the index says about them at that point: the latest description in full, then the details, up to about 6,000 characters. When a run has already marked which details tell the look, those come first.
  • Earlier looks. A look is often stated once and never repeated, because later descriptions are about who someone is. So the model also reads what the index said earlier about how they look, oldest first, after the entry. It is a few sentences chosen from their older descriptions and details, about 1,000 characters at most, and only from what you could see at that point. It is told that a look stated earlier stays true unless later material says it changed, and that the latest wins where two statements disagree.
  • The passages. Up to 900 words of the book's own paragraphs: first those the latest description and details cite, then the paragraphs those earlier looks cite (a quarter of the budget), then the paragraphs where the book first describes them, then other paragraphs that mention them. Where the book and the index disagree about a look, the book wins.
  • Their groups. The entries of the groups they are tied to or that their details name: a family, a house, a caste, an order. At most six, so the model can say who they are and what that looks like.
  • Where they belong. For a portrait, the settings where their events most often happen, the latest chapters counting most. It is a hint for the background, never something that must be drawn.
  • The book. The title, the author and the series name, for the period, place and genre. The model is told to draw nothing it knows of the book beyond what it was given.
  • Your note on the book's world, when you wrote one.

For a chapter picture or a moment it reads the moment and where it happens, what happened in the chapter before it, up to 600 words of the paragraphs that tell it, and the entries it names, at most six: the people and creatures first, then the places and things, then the people's groups. A thing counts as named when the event lists it or when the moment's summary uses one of its names, as you could see it at that point. Each person's or thing's entry is followed by its earlier looks, within a small share of the space. The model also says which of those people are in the picture, and calls every one of them by a label, whether or not they have a portrait (see Keeping one face).

When the moment happens at a place the index has an entry for, the model also reads that place's entry and its earlier looks, apart from the people's entries and within its own space, so the place never pushes a person out. A chapter picture uses the place of its main moment. The place counts only when you could see its entry at that point, and an earlier book's description only when you have finished that book. The model is told to draw the place as its entry describes it: what it is built of, its size and shape, how it is lit, its period. A thing in the picture is drawn as its entry describes it too. So the same inn or castle looks the same in every picture of it. Where the passages say more about the moment, they win. When the setting is only words, with no entry behind them, the model reads those words as before.

The model is told to describe only what this material says or plainly implies. It never gives a hair or eye colour, a garment or a hairstyle that the material does not state. Where the material is silent it says less, such as "formal dress" or "a man". It says who someone is, their people, rank, age and role, and what that looks like, since the image model knows nothing of the book. Where the material does not say where the subject is, the background stays simple.

A picture has no text in it, so the model never asks for visible writing, words, letters, numbers or runes, even on a body, a sign or a page. It says "marked" or "inscribed" instead. It also leaves out details too small to show at the picture's framing.

The labels that name figures in a scene prompt are never meant to be drawn. When a prompt uses "figure A" and so on, the prompt ends with one more sentence: the labels only tell the figures apart, and nothing is written in the picture, no letter, label, number or caption beside, above or on anyone. It is kept when you change the style or draw the same prompt again, and an older picture's prompt gets it when you redraw it with the same prompt. In a test of 48 scene pictures, no label was drawn as a letter either with or without the sentence, so it is a precaution that could not be shown to help. When a letter does show up, the check below finds it.

Where a picture shows a setting, the model also states the world's period and level of technology, and the plain marks of the place, such as what it is built of and how it is lit, as the book and the material make them plain. The image model knows nothing of the book, and a bare "a tavern" is drawn from its own default, which may be another age. So a prompt for a hall in a far-future story says so, and one for an inn in a nineteenth-century story says that. This applies to a scene's setting and to a portrait's one clause of background. When neither the book nor the material makes the period plain, the prompt says nothing about it.

An age is given only as the book gives it: a number, "a boy of sixteen", "young" or "old". When the book states it in two steps, the model works out a plain one, so "three years back I was thirty-two" becomes about thirty-five. It never ages someone with words the material does not use, such as "weathered", "grizzled" or "in his middle years". With no age in the material it writes "a man" or "a woman" and nothing that makes them look older. Scars and gauntness are described as marks, not as age.

Portraits

A portrait is a likeness, not a scene, and it is always a close picture from mid-chest up: head, neck, shoulders and upper chest, with the arms, hands, waist, legs and feet out of frame. Every portrait prompt opens with the same framing, put there by Lorekeeper rather than the model: "Close portrait cropped at mid-chest: the head, neck, shoulders and upper chest fill the picture (for a creature, its head and the top of its body), and the arms below the shoulders, the hands, the waist, the legs and the feet are out of frame. The figure is turned three-quarters toward the viewer, at rest, looking toward the viewer." It ends with "Nothing below mid-chest is shown." before the style sentence. The model is told the framing is given and never writes a pose of its own.

The model then describes only what shows from mid-chest up at that point: face, age, skin, hair, eyes, the scars, wounds, marks and tattoos on the face, neck and shoulders, their build as it shows in the neck and shoulders, and what they wear on the head, neck, shoulders and upper chest, such as a hood, a collar or a badge on the breast. It never mentions hands, anything they hold or carry, a belt, a sword at the hip, legs or boots, even when the book describes them, since naming them makes the image model draw the whole figure small to fit them in. There is no action, no gesture, no other figure and no event. The expression is one short phrase at most. Their people, caste or role stays, with what that looks like. The background is one short clause, from the places they usually are, or plain when none are known. It is never the room of a particular scene. A portrait shows the figure alive, or undead or transformed when they are at that point.

What the index says about a character's hands, weapons, gear and boots is not lost: scenes that show the character still read all of it. When a scene uses a chest-up portrait as a reference, the image model is told that the portrait shows only the head and upper chest, to take the face, hair, colouring and marks from it, and to draw the rest of the figure, arms, hands, dress below the chest and what they carry, as the prompt describes and at natural proportions.

Scene prompts also keep hands to what two hands can do. Each figure holds or uses at most two things, one in each hand, and anything else they carry is worn, stowed or set down. Figures are kept far enough apart to read as separate people, unless the moment is about them touching, and then the prompt says plainly where. Image models tend to add a third hand when a figure is given three things to hold, or to merge two figures that overlap.

Portraits drawn with the older half-length framing stay as they are. Redrawing one with Same prompt swaps the old framing sentence for the mid-chest one and adds "Nothing below mid-chest is shown." after the description; a prompt you typed yourself is drawn as you wrote it. The old description is kept, so it may still mention hands or a sword at the hip; choose Write a new prompt for one written for the mid-chest crop.

A portrait drawn before version 0.18.1 may still hold a pose or an action. Redraw it and choose Write a new prompt to get the fixed framing. Same prompt would keep the old pose. See Redraw, delete and undo.

The prompt never names a person or a creature, since a name invites the image model to draw what it remembers of the book. The names of peoples, houses, castes, places and things may stay, so "a young man of the Ashen caste" is fine. A prompt that names someone is written once more. If the second one still does, the name is replaced by a role and the prompt is checked again. The picture is skipped if it still fails.

Which text model writes a prompt, and which image model draws it, is up to you. See Who writes and who draws.

Your note on the book's world

The picture sheet has a line for your own note on the book's world, such as "Gaslit London in the 1890s". It is empty until you write one: choose Add a note about this book's world. There is one note per book edition, used in every style, and the model reads it with every picture of the book. A note changes the prompts written after it, so choose Write it again to use it in the prompt on screen. Lorekeeper no longer writes a world note of its own.

Keeping one face

Each picture is drawn on its own, so a face is kept by sending the image model a portrait to follow. Lorekeeper decides which portrait belongs to which figure by the character's entry in the index, not by a name, so a scene shows the right face whatever the character is called in the prompt.

Labels in a scene

Before the model writes a scene, every person and creature among its entries gets a neutral label on its entry, "figure A", "figure B" and so on up to "figure F". Those with a portrait you could see at that point come first, most connected first, then the rest, most connected first. The entry also says whether the image model will get a portrait of that figure. At most four portraits are sent, so a fifth or sixth figure with a portrait is labelled without one. When someone has no portrait yet at that point, their first portrait later in the book is used, but only one you have already reached and only if the book records no change of look in between.

The model is told to call every labelled figure it draws by its label the first time, followed by who they are in a few words ("figure A, a tidewarden in a dark travelling coat"), and by the label or a pronoun after that. For a figure with a portrait it does not describe their face, hair, eyes, skin or build, because the portrait gives those. For a figure without one it says how they look, only as far as the material states it. A figure it does not draw, such as someone heard, unseen or out of frame, is never labelled. The prompt still never holds a name.

The portraits are attached by what the text says, not by what the model listed. A portrait goes with the picture only for a label that appears in the prompt and has one, in label order. The model is checked: a label it was never given is written once more, and then dropped. A figure the model says is in the picture but never calls by its label gets the prompt written once more; if the second text still leaves the label out, the prompt is kept as it is.

The image provider gets a short note beside the prompt, not inside it: "Reference picture 1 is figure A. Reference picture 2 is figure B. Draw each labelled figure with the face, hair, colouring and build of their reference picture." When a reference is in another style, the note adds that only the likeness is to be taken, never the drawing style, numbered the same way. When a reference is a portrait, the note ends: "A reference picture that is a portrait may show only the head and upper chest: take the face, hair, colouring and marks from it, and draw the rest of the figure (body, arms, hands, dress below the chest, what they carry) as the prompt describes, at natural proportions for the whole body." The note is never part of the prompt you see or edit.

What an edit does

The labels are plain words in the prompt box, and what you leave in it is what is drawn. Take "figure B" out and the portrait for B is not sent and the note does not mention it. Type a label the sheet did not give, or one whose portrait is missing, and nothing is attached for it. If you rewrite a refused prompt with Rewrite it so it can be drawn, the new text keeps the labels of the figures it still draws and cannot add one. Same prompt in Redraw keeps the labels and their references and rebuilds the note from the labels still in the text. Drawing the scene in another style takes each label's portrait from that style's set, or a likeness from another style when it has none.

One face over a book

A portrait drawn later in the book is sent the same character's earlier portrait as a reference, whether or not their look changed. The note says so: "Reference picture 1 is this same figure earlier in the story: keep their face, hair, colouring and build, except where the prompt describes a change. Take the framing from the prompt, not from the reference picture." So a character does not turn blonde because the later notes stopped mentioning black hair. The earlier looks in the prompt keep the words, and the earlier portrait keeps the picture. A first portrait has no reference. The earlier portrait must be one you could see at that point, drawn by version 0.18.0 or later, and from a book you have finished.

Another style

The portrait can come from another style. When the style you are drawing in has no portrait of a character yet, the latest portrait of them in another style of the same book is sent instead. A comic-strip scene can then show the face of a digital-art portrait. The image model is told, beside the reference and not in your prompt, to take only the likeness from it: face, hair, build and marks, never the drawing style. Lorekeeper reads the other style's pictures and never changes them. The picture's record names the style each such reference came from.

In the picture sheet, under the prompt, a line says which figure is which: "Figure A is Orrin", with the portrait's small round thumbnail. The names come from what you have read, so someone you have not met yet shows as "Figure A" with no name. The lines follow the prompt box as you type. In the lightbox, How this was drawn lists the same lines for a drawn picture. In the sheet, the thumbnail is the reference the server attached when it wrote the prompt, and the name follows your position. In the lightbox, a thumbnail shows only while that portrait is itself one you could see, so a reference in another style shows the name without a picture, and both the name and the thumbnail follow your position as you move.

This gives a character you can recognise from picture to picture. It does not give the same face every time. A new pose or new light can still drift, and in a crowded scene the image model may follow a reference loosely or not at all.

Pictures drawn by an earlier version

Pictures drawn before version 0.18.0 stay as they are, and so do their prompts. Nothing is redrawn on its own. When you redraw one, Write a new prompt is the first choice, so it gets a prompt written the new way. To see which version your server runs, look at the foot of the section list in Settings, or point at the Lorekeeper name at the top of the rail. Earlier versions wrote notes on each character's look and an automatic world note. Those notes are kept in the set but no longer read, and a picture drawn from them is not sent as a reference for a new one. A world note you wrote yourself becomes your note on the book's world.

Who writes and who draws

A picture takes two models: a text model writes the prompt, and an image model draws it. Pictures split that work into five jobs, and each job has its own model.

JobWhat it doesDefault
Scene promptsWrites the prompts for chapter pictures and moments.The cheap model of the run's own cloud provider, else of the first one set up, such as Claude Haiku or Gemini Flash.
Portrait promptsWrites the prompts for portraits, places and things.The strong model of the same provider, such as Claude Sonnet or Gemini Pro.
Choosing callsPicks the moments of a chapter worth drawing, marks which details tell a look, and picks the sentences that state one.The cheap model, as for scene prompts.
Scene picturesDraws chapter pictures and moments.The image model chosen in Settings, else the provider's default.
PortraitsDraws portraits, places and things.The same model as scene pictures.

Portrait prompts get the strong model by default. A book has few portraits, and every scene sends them to the image model as references, so a better portrait pays off in every picture after it. It costs about a cent more a portrait. When the provider has no strong model, the scene writer writes the portraits too.

A text job can use any model of any cloud provider set up in Settings, the listed ones and those the provider reports itself. A local Ollama model is not offered. An image job can use any image model of Gemini or OpenAI. Claude cannot draw.

When a picture is refused, the quieter rewrite is written by the writer of its kind: the portrait writer for a portrait, the scene writer for a scene.

Where you choose

  • Settings, Pictures holds the defaults new pictures start from: Portraits drawn with under the image model, with Same as scenes first, and under Who writes the prompts the three text jobs: Scene prompts written by, Portrait prompts written by and Choosing calls by. Each list is grouped by provider and shows each model's price per million tokens, when it is known. Its first entry is the default, named. Like the other picture defaults, they are kept in this browser.
  • The build form and the Add pictures dialog have a closed Models group inside Pictures with the same five choices, starting from the Settings defaults. The estimate prices scene pictures with the scene model and portraits with the portrait model. It does not count what writing the prompts costs.
  • The picture sheet in the Reader has Prompt written by under the image model. It starts on the strong model for every kind of picture, whatever Settings says. Choosing another writer writes nothing. The new writer is used the next time you choose Write it again, pick another moment, or choose Rewrite it so it can be drawn. For a portrait, a place or a thing, the image model starts on the Settings portrait model.
  • The command line has a flag for each job. See From the command line.

In the lightbox, How this was drawn names the model that wrote the prompt, such as "Prompt written by Claude Sonnet 5.5". A picture drawn before this version has no such line. A redraw with the same prompt keeps the line. If you changed the prompt before drawing, the line still names the model that wrote the first text.

When a model lacks the quality

One quality applies to the whole run, but the portrait model may not offer it. Nano Banana 2 Lite has no Best, for example. Then the app draws at the nearest level the model has: Best becomes the highest it has, and otherwise the nearest level wins, a tie going up, so Draft becomes Standard. Each picture's record holds the level it was drawn at, and the estimate prices each kind at its own level. The command line refuses such a model instead, before any call.

When a run uses more than one text model, lorekeeper illustrate prints the text cost of each job at the end, so you can see what the stronger portrait writer cost. The web app keeps the same split in the run's report.

In a series

A later book builds on the earlier books' pictures. A character who already has a portrait is not drawn again unless the book records a change of look. Who counts as a main character is decided for each book on its own events, so a character who matters in book one but has a small part in book two gets no new portrait in book two. Scenes in book five use the portrait from book one, or its latest version. A prompt can use what the earlier books' index says about a character, and your note on an earlier book's world when this book has none.

What you see still follows what you have finished. A picture from an earlier book shows once you mark that book finished. Text from a book you have not finished stays hidden: your note on that book's world, and the prompt under "How this was drawn" when the prompt used that book, which instead says "How this was drawn is hidden until you finish" and the book's title. When you draw on demand, the prompt is written without anything from an unfinished book: none of its entries, passages, words or pictures.

Spoilers

Every picture belongs to a point in the book, the same kind of point a name, a detail or an event has. A chapter picture belongs to the end of its chapter, a moment to the moment, a comic to its last panel's moment, and a portrait to the description it was drawn from. The Reader shows a picture only once your position reaches that point. Before then it is not on the page, not in the counts and not behind the lightbox's arrows. Moving back hides it again.

A prompt is written only from what comes at or before its point: the passages, and the index as it stands there. The book's title and author are the one exception: the model may know the book, and it is told to use nothing beyond what it was given.

The prompt is checked before it is sent. It is written once more if it names a person or creature, uses a name the book has not used yet, reads with hindsight ("would later", "turns out", "now"), talks about the text rather than the picture, or states a family or romantic tie, such as "mother", "wife" or "lover", that nothing sent with it states. If the second try fails for any reason but a name, the picture is skipped. A word the book has not used yet is first checked by the cheap model, which answers only whether each use of it is a name. A period, a style or a nationality, such as "Victorian", "Gothic" or "English", is let through. The name of anyone or anything, real or made up, still counts as a name, and so does any word the model is unsure about. If that call fails, the word is treated as a name. The run report counts the words let through as "kept: a flagged word was not a name". The calls that mark which details describe a look, pick the sentences of an entry's notes that state a look, and screen a chapter's moments read further into the index, but they only choose: they return numbers, nothing they write goes into a prompt, and what they mark counts only from its own point. The sentence pick is made once for each character in each book and kept, by the cheap model. In a run it is made while the portraits are planned. The sheet makes it the first time it needs it, which is the one place a prompt waits on such a call, and falls back to the older descriptions if it fails. A picture is less exact than text, which helps, but a model that knows the book could still put something later into a scene without naming anyone, and no check catches that.

What is sent where

  • The text model writes the prompt. It gets what How a prompt is written lists: the index text visible at the picture's point, up to 900 words of the book's paragraphs, never past that point, the book's title, author and series, and your note on the book's world. If the book was built on the same cloud provider, it already read that text then.
  • The image provider gets only the written prompt and the reference portraits, with a short note saying which reference is which figure and, when a reference is in another style, that only its likeness is wanted. Your own reference pictures, when you add them, go with your notes (see Your own reference pictures). To fix a picture it gets the picture and the list of faults. It never gets the book's text or the EPUB.
  • The describing model, a text model that can see pictures, gets each of your reference pictures and its note only when the image model cannot take pictures.
  • The check for drawing faults, when you use it, sends a text model the picture, its prompt and the style's sentence, nothing else. Checking a fix sends it the picture before and after and the list of faults.

Pictures stay on your server, and so do your reference pictures. They are not part of share bundles or the community registry.

When a provider refuses

Image models refuse some scenes, mostly violence, gore and horror. The staking in Dracula and much of a war novel are likely to be refused. During a run, a refused picture is written once more as a quieter composition. If that is refused too, the picture is skipped and the report gives the provider's reason. A skipped picture is not tried again on resume. A skipped chapter picture leaves that chapter with no picture rather than a wrong one, though at "Every moment worth drawing" its other moments can still have theirs.

A picture you draw from the Reader is drawn from the prompt the sheet shows, and it is never rewritten without you. If it is refused, the sheet says so in one sentence with the provider's reason and keeps your prompt so you can change it. Under the reason, Rewrite it so it can be drawn asks the text model for a quieter picture of the same moment. It keeps who is there, where, the light and the mood. It shows what is hard to look at by implication: a figure covered, a face turned away, the moment before or after, the faces of those watching. It leaves out graphic detail, wounds and bodies shown dead, and it gives no age for anyone young. It never tries to trick the filter with misspellings, code words or another language. The new text goes into the prompt box with your style kept, and nothing is drawn until you read it and choose Draw. If that is refused too, the button is offered again. The rewrite reads the same material as the prompt, so it costs about a cent.

Drawing a whole book

There are two ways to draw a whole book.

  • When you build. Open Pictures on the build form, the section after Options, and tick Draw pictures. Choose the style, the chapter pictures ("The main moment of each chapter", "Every moment worth drawing" or "No chapter pictures"), the portraits ("Main characters", "Everyone with a part" or "No portraits"), the quality, and where to stop. The closed Models group holds the image provider and model and the other model choices (see Who writes and who draws). The form shows an estimate such as "About 52 pictures, about $1.75", or a range when the portraits are not planned yet (see Choosing chapters). Before the first build there is no index yet, so this is a rough count and there are no chapters to choose from. Pictures are drawn after the index is written, as the last stage of the run.
  • For a book that already has an index. The book page has Add pictures, or More pictures once it has some. It opens the same choices and starts a run that draws without building the index again. Here you can also choose the chapters.

Choosing chapters

In the book page's dialog, the chapter pictures choice is followed by the book's chapters, each with a box. Every chapter that has something to draw starts ticked. A line above them says how many are chosen, such as "12 of 28 chosen", with All and None beside it. Beside each chapter is what it would get at the level you chose: "1 picture", "3 pictures", "4 pictures, 1 already drawn" or "already drawn". A chapter with nothing to draw at that level says "nothing to draw" and cannot be ticked. Change the level and the numbers follow. The list is hidden at "No chapter pictures".

The chapter choice applies to chapter pictures and moments only. Portraits are chosen on their own and are never cut by it, so you can draw the main characters' portraits and the pictures of three chapters in one run.

The estimate follows every tick. It counts only the pictures the run would still draw: pictures already in the set are left out, since a run never draws them again. Until a run has checked a character's looks, the estimate cannot know how many times that character's look changes, so it counts one portrait and also the most a run could draw, three a book. The line then gives a range: "8 to 24 pictures, up to about 81 cents. Characters whose look changes get more than one portrait." Once every counted character has been checked, which a pictures run does as it plans, the line gives one number again. When everything you chose is drawn, the dialog says "Everything you chose is already drawn." and Start is off. Start is also off when nothing is chosen, no chapters (or "No chapter pictures") and "No portraits", with the sentence "Choose at least one chapter, or portraits." The build form turns Build off for the same reason when Draw pictures is ticked.

The Runs page shows "Drawing pictures" as the run's last phase, with a line such as "Pictures 12 of 52, $0.40 on pictures". A run can be stopped and resumed. A resume draws the rest and never draws a picture twice. If the drawing fails, the index stays as the run wrote it, with every picture drawn so far.

Drawing one picture

In the Reader, Draw a picture is a quiet button under a chapter's recap, at the end of a Timeline row (shown when you point at the row, or tap it on a touch screen), and under an entry's name. Any person, creature, group, place or thing can be drawn this way. In a series, the entry button is offered only for an entry that has appeared in the book you are reading, and it is not shown in edit mode. The button appears when an image provider is set up or the book already has pictures, and only when the server has the book file.

It opens a sheet. The sheet writes a prompt from what you have read, which takes a few seconds to half a minute and costs about a cent. For a chapter, it lists the moments you can draw. You can then:

  • change the prompt, or choose Write it again. Under the prompt, a line for each figure label tells you who "figure A" is; keep the label to keep that portrait as a reference;
  • pick a style from its preview (see Choosing a style), the shape, the quality, the provider and the model, all at once with no new prompt;
  • choose which text model writes the prompt under Prompt written by. It is used the next time a prompt is written;
  • add or change your note on the book's world;
  • add up to four pictures of your own for the image model to follow (see Your own reference pictures);
  • see the price, then choose Draw.

Drawing the same moment again in another style uses the same prompt, with no wait. Drawing an entry that already has a picture adds a new picture beside the old one. To swap a picture for a new one, use Redraw instead.

The picture is filed where it belongs in the book, not at your position, so it shows at the same place for every reader of that index. A chapter picture belongs to its chapter, and a moment to the moment.

A portrait, a place or a thing belongs to the earliest point where the book has already told its look, counting only what you have read. Its prompt is written only from what the index knew at that point. So a portrait you draw at 8% still shows when you go back to 5%, as long as its look was known there. Changing the style, or choosing Rewrite it so it can be drawn on the prompt as it was written, keeps that point. If you change the prompt yourself, it may hold something you read later, so the picture is filed at your position instead.

One character through the book so far

For a person or a creature, the sheet starts with What to draw. As they are now is the single portrait described above, at your position. Through the book so far draws the portraits a whole-book run would plan for this one character, up to your position: one at their first look, and one at each lasting change in how they look, at most three a book (see What is drawn). So at 100% you get the character as they looked at each stage, not only as they look at the end. Groups, places and things have only the single picture.

Nothing past your position is drawn. A change of look that comes later in the book is left for later: move on and choose it again, and only the new portrait is drawn. Each portrait's prompt is written at its own point in the book, from what the index says up to that point, never from what comes after. If the book describes their look only after their first description, and you have not reached that look yet, the first portrait is drawn from the first description instead.

In this mode the sheet shows no prompt, because each portrait gets its own. You choose the style, the quality, the provider and the model under Draw with and Model, and Prompts written by. The image model you pick draws every portrait of the run: it is the run's portrait image model, and the estimate prices the portraits with it. Prompts written by is the run's portrait writer, and it writes every portrait's prompt. Left as it is, it is the strong model, the default for portrait prompts (see Who writes and who draws). The line beside Start says what the run would draw, such as "3 portraits, about 10 cents.", and "1 more is already drawn." when some are. Portraits already drawn are never drawn again. Until the run has read how the character looks, the number can only be a range: "2 to 4 portraits, about 7 to 14 cents. The exact number is known once the run has read how they look." When there is nothing left to draw, the line says "Nothing new to draw for Lucy Westenra up to here." and Start is off. The count follows your position, and Start waits until it matches where you are.

Start queues a pictures run for that one character. The sheet closes with "Drawing Lucy Westenra through the book so far." and a Follow it in Runs link. The run stops at the cap you set under Settings, Pictures ($10 unless you changed it). The calls that only choose, such as reading how the character looks, use the cheap model. As portraits arrive, the open Reader picks them up: the entry shows the latest one you can see, the older ones move to "Earlier impressions", and scenes drawn after it use them to keep the face.

In a series the run also draws the character in the earlier books you have finished, each into that book's own pictures, when the server has that book's file. A book you have not finished is never drawn, and nothing from it goes into a prompt. Portraits drawn for a reader who has not finished every earlier book are kept for that reader only, as on-demand pictures are.

Chapter comics

A comic tells a chapter as one page of 2 to 6 panels, each showing one of its moments, in reading order. On the Timeline, each chapter of the book you are reading has Make a comic beside its title. It shows when you can draw pictures, not in edit mode, and only when you have reached at least two of the chapter's moments.

The page shows what you have reached. For a finished chapter that is the whole chapter. For the chapter you are in, it is the moments up to your position, and none after it. Small moments are never panels. A page has 2 to 6 panels. When the chapter has more moments than that, its turning points come first and the rest are picked evenly through the chapter, then all of them are put back in reading order. Lorekeeper chooses them itself, with no extra model call. If a pictures run has already picked the chapter's moments worth drawing, the panels come from those when at least two of them are reached, so the page can have fewer panels than the Timeline has moments.

The sheet lists the panels, numbered, each with its moment as the index tells it. Under them is the prompt: a line on the page's layout, then one line for each panel, then the style. You can change it, and the style, shape, quality and model, as for any picture. A comic starts as a tall page, and square and landscape are offered too. Each figure gets one label for the whole page, so a character keeps the same face from panel to panel, from their portrait when they have one.

Nothing is written into the picture: no speech balloons, captions or panel numbers. The words are the app's. When you open the comic, a numbered list under the picture gives each panel's moment, taken from the index at your position.

A comic belongs to its last panel's moment, so it shows once you reach that moment. If you change the prompt yourself, it is filed at your position instead, since your words may hold something you read later. In a series, such a page also stays hidden until every earlier book is finished. If you are behind the point a prompt was written for, that prompt is refused rather than filed early.

The latest comic of a chapter sits at the top of its Timeline row, under the label "Comic". The row shows the top of the page; open it to see the whole page. The arrows step back through the chapter's older comics. Make a comic again adds a new page beside the old one, and Redraw replaces it. A comic is not a chapter picture: it does not show on the Chapters tab and is not counted in "N drawn".

Fixing one panel

Often every panel is right but one. Open the comic and fix that panel alone: the rest of the page stays as it is. In the numbered list under the picture, each panel has a Fix panel button. On a screen with a mouse, a Fix panel N button also shows at the top right of each panel when you point at the picture or tab to it. It needs the page's panel boxes, which comics drawn from this version on carry. An older comic has only the list buttons. On a touch screen the list is the way in. Both are offered where Fix is: when the image provider that drew the page is set up on this server.

The panel form is headed "Panel N". It holds the panel's text, which you can change, a one-line note, the price of one picture and Fix it. Fix it stays off until you change the text or type a note. The text starts as what the prompt said for that panel. It is empty when the prompt is hidden from you, for example in a series until the earlier books are finished, or when an older comic's prompt cannot be split into panels.

Lorekeeper asks the model that drew the page to change only that panel and leave the others exactly as they are. The model sees the whole page, so faces and style stay consistent from panel to panel. Then Lorekeeper takes only that panel's box from the edited page and pastes it into your original, with its border. Every other panel keeps its original pixels. If the boxes cannot be found on both pages, or they do not line up, the whole edited page is kept instead. The panel text you typed is yours: it is not rewritten or checked against the book. It replaces that panel's line in the page's prompt, so a Same prompt redraw draws what you asked for.

The result is shown as for any fix (see Seeing what the fix did): the sentence on what was put right, Before and After, Keep the fix and Keep the original. A panel that came back unchanged is not kept. Your page stays, and the form comes back as you typed it. If you close the lightbox before choosing, the fix stands with "Panel fixed" and Undo. How this was drawn lists it as "Fixed panel 3: " followed by your new text, cut to 120 characters, or your note, with its date. A panel fix costs one picture plus a check, and it is on demand only: runs and the estimate never include it.

A comic costs one picture, plus the prompt. The strong model writes it, which came to about 5 cents a page on Claude Sonnet. Comics are drawn one at a time from the Reader only. A whole-book run never draws them, and the estimate does not count them.

Your own reference pictures

When you draw a picture yourself, you can give the image model pictures of your own to follow: a photo of a dress, a house you have in mind, a sword you like. This works for every picture you start in the Reader: a chapter picture, a moment, a comic, a portrait, a place or a thing, and a redraw. A whole-book run never uses them.

Adding them

The picture sheet has a Reference pictures row under the prompt and the figure lines. Choose Add reference pictures, or drop files on the row on a wide screen. On a phone there is only the button. You can add up to four pictures, and the button goes once you have four. Each must be a PNG, JPEG or WebP file of at most 8 MB, with each side 256 to 8192 pixels, the same rules as a style's picture. The server checks the file itself, not its name. A file that breaks a rule is refused with a sentence under the row.

Each picture is sent to your server as soon as you choose it, with a small spinner on its thumbnail. Draw is off while a picture is still uploading, and the sheet says "Uploading your pictures…". Each picture has a remove button and a field, What to take from it, for one line of up to 120 characters, such as "this dress" or "this is the house". The note is optional, but it is how the image model knows what to take from the picture. Who or what it is for is the next choice, Applies to.

Applies to

Each picture has an Applies to choice under its note. For a chapter picture, a moment or a comic, it offers:

  • The whole scene, the default. The picture is for the scene as a whole, as before this choice existed.
  • one choice for each labelled figure the prompt uses now, such as "Figure A, Mina". The name comes only from what you have read, so a figure you have not met yet reads "Figure A". The picture is then for that figure alone.

For a portrait, a place or a thing, it offers The whole picture and the subject itself: its name, or "The person drawn", "This place" or "This thing" when you have not read its name yet. When a scene's prompt labels no figure, there is nothing to choose and the choice is hidden.

The figure choices follow the prompt as you type. If you edit the prompt so a figure's label goes, a picture tied to that figure falls back to the whole scene, with one quiet line under it: "Figure B is no longer in the prompt, so this picture now applies to the whole scene." Put the label back and your choice comes back too. The server applies the same rule when it draws, and keeps a figure only when it is a person or a creature. If it had to fall back, the sheet says so once after drawing.

The lightbox shows the choice under each note in How this was drawn, such as "For figure A, Mina" or "For Mina", named from what you have read, and nothing for the whole scene. A redraw with Same prompt keeps each picture's choice. Write a new prompt starts with the choices you made, and a figure the new prompt no longer labels falls back to the whole scene as above.

Name the thing, and let Applies to say who. Write "this dress", not "the woman's dress". In tests, tying a picture to one figure kept its note off everyone else: a note left on the whole scene put a dress on the wrong figure, and the same note tied to figure A never did. But the note still wins on the figure it is tied to. A note that names the other sex changes that figure: "use this for the woman's dress", tied to a man, drew him as a woman in 8 tests of 8. In a later check, a plain "use this dress", tied to a man, kept him a man.

The pictures stay in the sheet when you change the style, the shape, the quality, the model or the moment. Closing the sheet drops them, and asks first if you added any.

How they are sent

A quiet line under the row says what happens to your pictures with the image model you chose. Every listed Gemini and OpenAI model takes pictures, so today the line reads "Sent to Nano Banana 2.1 as pictures. They go to Gemini with this picture."

The image model gets its pictures in this order: the portraits that keep the figures' faces (and a place's or thing's picture, when one is sent), then yours in the order you added them, then the style's own picture last, when the style sends one. A call holds at most six pictures. When yours do not fit beside the rest, Lorekeeper leaves out pictures to make room: first a place's or thing's picture, then the portrait of the last labelled figure. It never leaves out one of yours or the style's picture. A figure whose portrait was left out is drawn from the prompt's words alone, and after drawing the sheet says "1 character picture was left out to make room for yours."

Beside the prompt, never inside it, the image model is told which pictures are yours and what you said about each: "Reference picture 3 is from the person: this dress." A picture with no note gets "draw what the prompt describes with its help". A picture tied to a figure says so: "Reference picture 3 is from the person and is only for figure B: this dress. Use it for figure B alone; nothing else about figure B, and nothing about any other figure, changes." A picture for a portrait's, place's or thing's subject says it "is for the subject of this picture". The note then says once: "Take only what the person says from their pictures, never their drawing style; where their note and the prompt disagree about that thing, draw it as their picture shows." When a picture is tied to a figure, that sentence ends ", but a picture that is only for one figure changes that thing on that figure alone." Your words go as you wrote them, with no check, the same as a prompt you write yourself. The text model that writes the prompt never sees your pictures or your notes.

Your picture wins over the prompt. For the thing your note names, the image model follows your picture, even where the prompt says otherwise. Without that rule, a test drew the prompt's nightgown and the prompt's grey stone house and ignored the pictures. With it, both scenes drew the dress and the brick house from the pictures. The other side is that a note that does not fit the scene can change the scene. In one test, a note about a woman's dress turned the scene's man into a woman. So name only the thing in the note and choose the figure with Applies to (see Applies to).

In tests the drawing kept the chosen style, though every reference was a photograph, and labelled figures kept their portraits' faces.

When the image model cannot take pictures

An image model may be marked as taking no pictures. None of the listed models is today, so this is a fallback. With such a model, each of your pictures is first described in one or two sentences by a text model that can see pictures, the same one Describe it for me uses for a style (see A picture of the style). The description says what the picture shows that your note asks for: shapes, colours, materials, clothing, build. It never names a person, a place, a brand or an artist, and never describes the drawing style. Each description goes at the end of the prompt, before the style sentence, as "Also: this dress: a floor-length dress of deep emerald green velvet…". A picture tied to a figure says which: "Also, for figure B only: this dress: …". The prompt in the picture's record is the text that was sent, descriptions included.

Nothing is sent as a picture to such a model: not yours, not the figures' portraits, not the style's picture, whose words are already in the style sentence. The line under the row says so, and gives the price, which is less than a cent a picture on Claude Sonnet. A picture is described once for each note. A redraw, or another drawing with the same picture and note, reuses the description. When no model that can see pictures is set up, the line says your pictures can't be used, and Draw goes without them.

What is kept, and for how long

Your pictures are kept with the picture they drew. In the lightbox, How this was drawn lists them under Your reference pictures: a thumbnail that opens the file in a new tab, your note, and "Described as: …" when the picture was described. Each has a remove button, with Undo in the message that confirms it. Removing one does not change the drawn picture. It only changes what a redraw uses, and the list says so.

Redraw reuses them. Same prompt draws again with the same pictures and notes, and the message says "with your 2 reference pictures". Write a new prompt opens the sheet with them already in the row, where you can remove some, add others or change the notes. Fix never sends them, since a fix keeps the picture's own look, but the fixed picture keeps the list for a later redraw.

The files are kept on your server, one folder per book edition, shared by every style of that book. The same file uploaded twice is stored once. A file that no picture lists and no undo entry still needs is deleted after a day, so a picture you added and never drew goes then. Using a picture, by drawing or redrawing with it, starts the day again. Removing every picture of a book in Settings removes its reference pictures too, and Undo brings them back. Settings counts them in the book's size.

Your pictures leave the server only for the image provider that draws with them, or the text model that describes them. They are never part of an index, the /v0 protocol, a share bundle or the community registry. You can use them with a community or example index, as you can draw pictures for one.

Redraw, delete and undo

Click a picture to open it full size. The arrows step through the other pictures of that chapter or entry that you can see, or for a comic, the chapter's other comics. How this was drawn shows the prompt, which portrait was used for each figure, the style, the model that drew it, the model that wrote the prompt, the quality, the cost, the date, and any fixes (see Fixing a drawing fault). Delete asks first.

Redraw offers two choices:

  • Write a new prompt closes the picture and opens the picture sheet, titled "Redraw", for the same chapter, moment or entry. The prompt is written from what you have read so far, as for a new picture, and you can change it, the style and the rest before you choose Draw.
  • Same prompt draws the saved prompt again with the same references and figure labels, with an optional note put first, such as "in daylight, closer to the face".

For a picture drawn before version 0.18.0, Write a new prompt comes first, since its prompt was written the older way. For a newer picture, Same prompt comes first. Either way the new picture takes the old one's place. If you change the style on the sheet, the new picture goes into that style's set instead and the old one stays where it is. Write a new prompt is offered only when an image provider is set up and you can see what the picture shows at your position. Otherwise Redraw goes straight to the same prompt.

Each change can be undone from the message that confirms it. Undo uses the same history as hand edits and duplicate merges, and it reverts only the pictures that change touched, so pictures drawn since are kept.

Fixing a drawing fault

Image models often get things wrong that a reader spots: a third hand, a melted face, a sword passing through the hand that holds it, a chair with a leg missing. Fix sends the picture back to the model that drew it, with a list of what to correct, and keeps the rest of the picture as it is.

Open the picture and choose Fix, beside Redraw. It is offered where Redraw is, when the image provider that drew the picture is set up on this server. The lightbox says "Looking for drawing faults…" while a text model looks at the picture. It is the strong model of the first cloud provider set up here whose strong model can see pictures, such as Claude Sonnet, and a cheap one when no strong model can. A check costs about a cent on Claude Sonnet.

The check is meant to be critical. It first counts, figure by figure, the heads, arms, hands and legs it sees, and then reports what a careful viewer would find wrong on a second look. A fault it can point to is reported even when it is small. It looks for:

  • an extra or missing head, arm, hand or leg;
  • a limb joined to the wrong body or in the wrong place, or one that starts or ends nowhere;
  • a joint bent the way it cannot bend;
  • bodies merged into one another;
  • a head or body far out of proportion to the rest;
  • a distorted or melted face, such as eyes at different heights or a doubled feature;
  • a hand with wrong fingers;
  • an object merged into a body, or passing through a hand or a body;
  • a held thing floating with no grip, or a hand gripping nothing it should hold;
  • a doubled or broken object, such as a sword with two blades or a candle melting into the table;
  • furniture or a building that could not stand;
  • figures at an impossible scale to each other or to the room;
  • one person drawn twice;
  • letters, words, numbers, a label, a caption or a signature drawn in the picture, such as a letter beside a figure, a caption strip, or readable writing on a sign or a page;
  • a picture plainly in another style than the one chosen, such as a photograph or a full-colour painting where the style is ink.

Each fault is major, seen at a glance, or minor, seen on a second look. Writing is major when you can read it at a glance and minor when it is small. Marks that only suggest writing, such as an illegible scribble on a page, are not a fault. A style fault is listed only when it is major: a reader would say at a glance that it is the wrong kind of picture. A slight drift, such as a wash of colour in an ink drawing, is left alone, and with no style known the check never reports one. There is at most one. Apart from that it never judges taste, mood, colour, composition or likeness, nor a limb cut off by the frame or hidden behind something, foreshortening, or rough hands in a loose style. A part that is only sketchy, vague or hard to make out is not a fault either: a fault is something drawn wrong. It judges how things are drawn, not whether the picture follows its prompt. It reads the prompt too, so a figure the prompt describes as missing an arm is not a fault.

The lightbox names the model that looked, such as "Claude Sonnet 5.5 found 2 drawing faults." Each fault is a field you can change, with where it is in the picture as a quiet line under it, and a quiet tag: "minor" on a minor fault, "text" on writing and "style" on a wrong style. Remove any you disagree with, or choose Add something to fix to say what it missed. If it finds nothing, it says so and still gives you an empty field, so you can say what is wrong yourself. There is also an optional note for anything else. The price of one picture at the picture's own model and quality comes before Fix it.

Fix it asks the same provider and model, at the same quality and shape, to edit the picture: keep the composition, the faces, the clothes, the colours, the light and the style, and correct only what the list names. A writing fault is removed and painted over with what was behind it. A style fault asks for the whole picture to be redrawn in the chosen style, keeping the composition, framing, faces, clothing and light. Every fault you leave in the list is fixed, minor ones too. The prompt is not changed.

Seeing what the fix did

Image models sometimes send a picture back without the change, so every fix is checked before it is called fixed. The same model that looked for the faults gets the picture before and after the fix and says, fault by fault, whether it was put right, partly put right or not at all, and whether the fix drew anything new that is wrong, letters included. Writing counts as fixed when none is left, and a style fault when the picture now looks like the style.

  • When the picture came back the same, or nothing on the list was put right even in part, the fix is not kept. Your picture stays, the lightbox says "The model sent the picture back without the fix, so your picture is kept. Try again, or say it differently." and your list and note are still there. The edit is still billed.
  • Otherwise the fixed picture takes the old one's place, and a sentence under it says what changed: "Fixed all 3.", or "Fixed 2 of 3. Still wrong: the left hand has six fingers.", or "Partly fixed: …". When the fix drew something new that is wrong, it adds "The fix added: …". When the check after the fix failed, it says "Fixed. The result couldn't be checked."

Under that sentence, Before and After switch the picture on screen between the original and the fix, so you can compare them. Keep the fix closes the result. Keep the original undoes the fix and shows the old picture again. If you close the lightbox or step to another picture without choosing, the fix stays, with the message "Picture fixed" and Undo.

How this was drawn lists each fix with its date and how much it put right, such as "Fixed: the head is far too small for the body; the sword passes through the hand (1 of 2 put right)." If the provider refuses, the lightbox says so with its reason and keeps your list.

An edit is not perfect. It keeps the picture much better than a redraw does, but many faults survive it. In a test of 8 fixes, Claude Sonnet found 1 fully fixed, 5 partly fixed and 2 unchanged. You can fix the same picture again.

Checking every new picture

Under Settings, Pictures, Check pictures for drawing faults runs the same check on every new picture: in a run, from the picture sheet, and on a prompt you wrote yourself. It is off unless you turn it on. In a run, this check uses the model of the choosing calls, a cheap one unless you chose otherwise. A picture drawn from the picture sheet is checked by the cheap model of the first cloud provider set up here that can see pictures. Either way, the same model checks its own fixes. A picture with a major fault is fixed once, and only its major faults go in the list. When the fix changed nothing, the original picture is kept. A picture with only minor faults is kept as drawn. Nothing is fixed a second time, and your prompt is never rewritten. The picture sheet says "Redrawn: the first try had a drawing fault." after a fix.

On Claude Haiku the check costs about 6 cents per 100 pictures (a little more than before it also looked for writing and style), and checking a fix about as much again. Each fix costs one more picture at the picture's own price. On a test of 20 scenes, Haiku found a fault in most of them, but a major one in 4, so about one picture in five is fixed and a run costs about a fifth more. The estimate on the build form and in the book page's dialog includes it, and the run's cap counts it: a fix the cap has no room for is skipped. The run report says how many pictures were checked and fixed, how many fixes were refused, and how many changed nothing.

The check needs a text model that can see pictures. The models of Claude, OpenAI and Gemini can. Groq and DeepSeek cannot, and neither can a local Ollama model. Without one, pictures are drawn unchecked, and the run log says so once. Fix in the lightbox then still works on the list you write, and the fix is kept without being checked.

Showing and hiding pictures

When a book has pictures, the Reader's head row has a Pictures switch, and a style menu when the book has been drawn in more than one style. On a phone both are in the ⋯ menu. Your choices are remembered in this browser. With pictures off, no picture shows, and the Draw a picture buttons stay. A book with no pictures and no image provider looks as it always did.

On an entry

An entry with a picture you can see opens on it: the latest portrait fills the top of the entry, with the name over its foot (see An entry). When you can see two or more portraits of a character, they follow under a heading such as "3 looks so far". The portraits run oldest to newest, each captioned with the chapter it is from, such as "Chapter XIV", the way the Chapters tab names it. The latest is marked "Current". For a place or a thing the heading says "2 pictures so far". Tap a portrait to open it full size; the arrows step through the others.

In a series, the pictures have one row for each book, oldest book first, headed with the book, such as "Book 1, A Study in Scarlet". It holds every portrait of the book you are reading and of each book you have finished, not only the latest of each. The cards under "Through the series" no longer repeat them. A card for the book you are reading that has nothing else new points to them instead: "2 portraits, shown above".

It shows only portraits you could see at your position. A portrait from later in the book is not shown and not counted, and moving back takes it away again. "Earlier impressions" holds only earlier descriptions, in words.

Settings

Under Settings, Cloud models has an Images column. It names each provider's image model and its price for one picture, or says "Can't draw".

Pictures holds the defaults that new pictures start from: the style, the quality, where a run stops, the image provider and model, the portrait image model, the three text models that write the prompts and make the choosing calls, whether to check new pictures for drawing faults (see Checking every new picture), and whether to draw style previews for new books (see Style previews from your book). From the command line

lorekeeper illustrate draws a set of pictures for an index into art/<textHash>/<style>/ beside it. It never writes the index, and a second run resumes where the first stopped. Pass the EPUB the index was built from:

uv run --extra claude lorekeeper illustrate examples/dracula/dracula.json \
  --epub corpus/pg345/pg345.epub --style ink --level main --provider gemini --quality draft --cap 3

--level all draws every moment worth drawing, --level none draws no chapter pictures, only portraits, --portraits all draws everyone with a part instead of the main characters, --portraits none (or --no-portraits) leaves out the portraits, --chapters 4-6 draws chapter pictures only for those table of contents entries (see lorekeeper toc) and portraits only up to the end of the last of them, including people met earlier, and --limit N draws at most N pictures. The image key comes from GEMINI_API_KEY or OPENAI_API_KEY, or from --api-key-file. The text models come from Claude when ANTHROPIC_API_KEY is set, else from the image provider; --text-provider chooses another. Its cheap model writes the scene prompts and makes the choosing calls, or --text-model when given, and its strong model writes the portrait prompts. Each job has its own flag: --writer, --portrait-writer and --picker take PROVIDER:MODEL, or PROVIDER alone for that provider's cheap model (its strong one for --portrait-writer), and --portrait-model takes the image model for portraits, places and things as PROVIDER:MODEL or a model of --provider. A portrait model that lacks --quality is refused. A provider other than --provider takes its key from its environment variable. For example, to have Sonnet write the portraits and Nano Banana Pro draw them:

uv run --extra claude lorekeeper illustrate examples/dracula/dracula.json \
  --epub corpus/pg345/pg345.epub --level none --provider gemini --quality standard --cap 1 \
  --portrait-writer claude:claude-sonnet-5-5 --portrait-model gemini:gemini-3-pro-image

For a series, pass the books in reading order with one --epub each and --series, or pass earlier books with --prior to read their pictures and entries without drawing them.

lorekeeper draw draws one picture from a prompt you give, which is a quick way to try a provider or a style:

uv run lorekeeper draw "A ruined castle on a crag at dusk, one lit window." -o castle --provider gemini --shape landscape

It writes the file with the extension of the format the provider sent and prints the model, size and cost. --ref FILE adds a picture to follow, up to six.

For developers

Pictures are not part of the index format or the /v0 protocol, and the specification does not cover them. In this version they belong to the reference implementation: the web app, lorekeeper illustrate and lorekeeper draw. Each set is a folder per book edition and style, with an art.json manifest that lists each picture with its kind, what it shows, its from anchor, the prompt as sent, the provider, the model and the cost. A scene also has figures, [{"label", "entity", "ref"}]: each label in its prompt, the entity it stands for and the reference sent for it (or none). Its references are picture ids of the same set, or {"set": slug, "id": id} for a likeness taken from another style. A comic has kind: "comic", its chapter as the target, and panels, the event ids it shows in panel order. Its from is the last panel's anchor. The prompt and soften routes answer panels too (null for any other kind). A comic needs the reader's position p: without it the routes answer 422 bad-position, and with fewer than two of the chapter's moments reached, 422 too-few-moments. A picture drawn with replaces took the place of the picture with that id. A comic drawn from this version on also has grid, its panel boxes in reading order as [x0, y0, x1, y1] fractions of the page (found from the white gutters, so it needs the Pillow library, and is absent without it), and panelTexts, one text per panel. A fixed picture keeps its prompt and adds to fixes, [{"faults", "note", "created", "cost", "provider", "model", "verified"}], newest last, where each fault is {"what", "where", "severity", "kind"}, severity is "major" or "minor" and kind is "drawing", "text" or "style" (missing means "drawing"; any other value in a fix request is 422 bad-fix). verified is {"fixed", "partly", "of", "results", "new"}: how many faults were put right and partly put right, out of how many, one {"n", "fixed"} per fault numbered from 1 with "yes", "partly" or "no", and up to 3 faults the fix added. It is null when the fix could not be checked. A fix's cost includes its check. A fix of one comic panel adds panel (its number from 1), pasted (whether only that panel was taken from the edited page), and text when the panel text changed; the picture's prompt then holds the new panel text. The route is POST /api/library/{key}/art/{id}/panel with {k, text, note, faults, p, finished}. It answers as the fix route does, plus 422 not-comic and bad-panel, and in verified the first result is the panel's own change. A picture the automatic check found a fault in also has check, {"faults", "fixed"}, plus verified when a fix was tried. fixed is false when only minor faults were found or the fix changed nothing. The web app's routes are POST /api/library/{key}/art/{id}/check, which answers {faults, cost, model} and writes nothing, and POST /api/library/{key}/art/{id}/fix with {faults, note, p, finished}, which answers as a redraw does, plus verified and beforeUrl. A fix that changed nothing answers 422 with code: "unchanged", verified and fixCost, and files nothing. GET /api/library/{key}/art/{id}/before?p=&finished= serves the picture a fix or redraw replaced, gated like the picture, until that change is undone. A picture drawn with your own reference pictures has userRefs, [{"sha", "ext", "note", "description", "appliesTo"}] in the order sent, where sha is the SHA-256 of the file, note is "" for none, description is there only when the picture was described in words, and appliesTo is there only when the picture was not for the whole scene: "subject" or a figure label "A" to "F", as used after any fallback (a label maps to an entity only through the record's own figures); references still lists only pictures of the sets. A picture whose figure portraits were left out to make room has refsLeftOut. The files live in art/<textHash>/refs/<sha>.<ext> beside the sets. The routes are POST /api/library/{key}/art/refs (multipart file, answers {sha, ext, width, height, bytes, url}, 413 too-large, 422 bad-image), GET /api/art/{textHash}/refs/{sha}, refs: [{sha, note, appliesTo}] on /art/draw and /art/{id}/redraw (at most four distinct uploads, notes of at most 120 characters, appliesTo missing or null for the whole scene, else "scene", "subject" or one letter A to F, else 422 bad-refs; a draw reply lists in appliesFallback the shas of pictures whose figure left the prompt and went to the whole scene, and the filed record never carries it; a redraw without refs reuses the record's, and [] drops them), and PUT /api/library/{key}/art/{id}/refs with {refs}, which changes the list, appliesTo included, without drawing and can be undone. A draw that needs a description and finds no model that can see pictures answers 409 no-vision-model. The Reader compares a picture's from anchor with the reader's position exactly as it compares any other item's.
