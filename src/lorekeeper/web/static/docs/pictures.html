Pictures

Lorekeeper can draw pictures for a book: one for each chapter, more for the moments worth seeing, and a portrait of each main character. The Reader shows a picture only once you have read up to the point it belongs to.

Pictures are off until you ask for them. They need an image provider, which is a Gemini or OpenAI key, and a cloud text model to write the prompts. Claude cannot draw, but it can write the prompts while Gemini or OpenAI draws. Pictures are kept beside the index, never inside it, so an index you share or install stays text only.

What can be drawn

  • Chapter pictures. One picture for the main moment of a chapter. It sits under the chapter's title on the Chapters tab.
  • Moments. A picture for another moment of a chapter, shown at the left of its event on the Timeline.
  • Portraits. One for each of the book's main characters: the people and creatures the story is most about, up to 12 a book. A character counts as main when it is named in at least one in twenty of the book's events, and in at least 3. Small moments do not count. When more than 12 qualify, the 12 named most are drawn. "Everyone with a part" draws a wider cast: every person and creature with a description who is named in two of the book's events, small moments not counted, or who comes up often enough to get a description written from every passage about them. In Dracula that is 32 of the 51 characters with a description. The portrait sits beside "Who they are" in the entry, and a small round one replaces the type mark in the Cast list. When the character's look changes for good, a new portrait is drawn and the old one moves to "Earlier impressions". A lasting wound or scar, a lost eye, a disguise, a body remade or a new way of dressing all count. A death alone does not. A character has at most three portraits per book. You can still draw a portrait of anyone else from their entry in the Reader.
  • Places and things. These are never drawn ahead of time, but you can draw any of them on demand from its entry.

To plan the portraits of a run, a cheap text call reads every detail and description the index holds about a character in the book. For each one it says whether it tells how they look, and whether it records a lasting change of their look. It only marks items. Nothing it writes goes into a prompt, and an item counts only from its own point in the book. A first portrait is drawn at the first item that describes the look, else at the first description, and a new version at each change, at most three a book. The call works the same way for any book, since it reads the index's own words rather than a list of look words. It is made only for a run. Drawing one portrait from the Reader never waits for it.

How many pictures a chapter gets

Before drawing a chapter, a cheap text call reads the chapter's events and picks the ones that can be drawn: a moment with a place, people or things in view. "Harker finds every door locked" can be drawn. "Harker concludes he is a prisoner" cannot. Small moments are never drawn. That count is the most pictures the chapter can have.

When you build, you choose one of two levels:

  • The main moment of each chapter draws one picture per chapter. It is the chapter's turning point when one can be drawn, else the moment the chapter recap says most about, else the recap itself.
  • Every moment worth drawing draws all the moments the check picked.

A chapter with no events gets no picture. The Reader's picture sheet makes no such call: for a chapter not checked yet, it offers every moment that is not a small one. In the Reader, the picture sheet for a chapter says how many moments it has so far and how many are drawn, for example "4 moments in this chapter so far, 1 drawn", and you can draw the rest one at a time.

Styles

Nine styles come with Lorekeeper: Digital art, Comic strip, Anime, Realism, Hand-drawn, Oil painting, Epic sci-fi, Watercolour and Ink. Each is one sentence added to every prompt, such as "Black ink drawing, fine cross-hatching, strong contrast, aged paper." for Ink. Choose Custom to write your own sentence, for example "linocut, two colours". Describe the look, never an artist's name.

Each style is a separate set of pictures. You can draw a book in Ink and again in Watercolour, and the Reader's style menu switches between them.

The style is not part of what the text model writes. A prompt is the model's description, then the style sentence, then a sentence asking for no text in the picture. So changing the style in the picture sheet is instant: the sheet swaps the style sentence and writes nothing again. If you edited the prompt, the old style sentence is replaced where it still stands at the end. If you took it out, the sheet leaves your text alone and says that the style you chose is not in the prompt. Shape, quality, provider and model are instant too.

How a prompt is written

Each picture's prompt is written by one call to a text model. It reads the index and the book as they stand at the picture's point, each part marked with where it comes from, and writes a description: 60 to 150 words for a scene, 50 to 110 for a portrait. For a portrait, a place or a thing it reads:

  • The entry. What the index says about them at that point: the latest description in full, then the details, up to about 6,000 characters. When a run has already marked which details tell the look, those come first.
  • Earlier looks. A look is often stated once and never repeated, because later descriptions are about who someone is. So the model also reads what the index said earlier about how they look, oldest first, after the entry. It is a few sentences chosen from their older descriptions and details, about 1,000 characters at most, and only from what you could see at that point. It is told that a look stated earlier stays true unless later material says it changed, and that the latest wins where two statements disagree.
  • The passages. Up to 900 words of the book's own paragraphs: first those the latest description and details cite, then the paragraphs those earlier looks cite (a quarter of the budget), then the paragraphs where the book first describes them, then other paragraphs that mention them. Where the book and the index disagree about a look, the book wins.
  • Their groups. The entries of the groups they are tied to or that their details name: a family, a house, a caste, an order. At most six, so the model can say who they are and what that looks like.
  • Where they belong. For a portrait, the settings where their events most often happen, the latest chapters counting most. It is a hint for the background, never something that must be drawn.
  • The book. The title, the author and the series name, for the period, place and genre. The model is told to draw nothing it knows of the book beyond what it was given.
  • Your note on the book's world, when you wrote one.

For a chapter picture or a moment it reads the moment and where it happens, what happened in the chapter before it, up to 600 words of the paragraphs that tell it, and the entries of the people it names and of their groups, at most six. Each person's entry is followed by their earlier looks, within a small share of the space. The model also says which of those people are in the picture, and labels those who have a portrait (see Keeping one face).

The model is told to describe only what this material says or plainly implies. It never gives a hair or eye colour, a garment or a hairstyle that the material does not state. Where the material is silent it says less, such as "formal dress" or "a man". It says who someone is, their people, rank, age and role, and what that looks like, since the image model knows nothing of the book. Where the material does not say where the subject is, the background stays simple.

Where a picture shows a setting, the model also states the world's period and level of technology, and the plain marks of the place, such as what it is built of and how it is lit, as the book and the material make them plain. The image model knows nothing of the book, and a bare "a tavern" is drawn from its own default, which may be another age. So a prompt for a hall in a far-future story says so, and one for an inn in a nineteenth-century story says that. This applies to a scene's setting and to a portrait's one clause of background. When neither the book nor the material makes the period plain, the prompt says nothing about it.

An age is given only as the book gives it: a number, "a boy of sixteen", "young" or "old". When the book states it in two steps, the model works out a plain one, so "three years back I was thirty-two" becomes about thirty-five. It never ages someone with words the material does not use, such as "weathered", "grizzled" or "in his middle years". With no age in the material it writes "a man" or "a woman" and nothing that makes them look older. Scars and gauntness are described as marks, not as age.

Portraits

A portrait is a likeness, not a scene. Every portrait prompt opens with the same framing, put there by Lorekeeper rather than the model: "Portrait, head and shoulders to half length, the figure turned three-quarters toward the viewer, at rest, looking toward the viewer." The model is told the framing is given and never writes a pose of its own.

The model then describes only how the figure looks at that point: face, age, build, skin, hair, eyes, scars and wounds they carry, tattoos, and what they wear and carry on them, such as a sword at the hip. Their hands are empty. There is no action, no gesture, no other figure and no event. The expression is one short phrase at most. Their people, caste or role stays, with what that looks like. The background is one short clause, from the places they usually are, or plain when none are known. It is never the room of a particular scene. A portrait shows the figure alive, or undead or transformed when they are at that point.

A portrait drawn before version 0.18.1 may still hold a pose or an action. Redraw it and choose Write a new prompt to get the fixed framing. Same prompt would keep the old pose. See Redraw, delete and undo.

The prompt never names a person or a creature, since a name invites the image model to draw what it remembers of the book. The names of peoples, houses, castes, places and things may stay, so "a young man of the Ashen caste" is fine. A prompt that names someone is written once more. If the second one still does, the name is replaced by a role and the prompt is checked again. The picture is skipped if it still fails.

The sheet writes its prompt with the strong model of the first cloud provider set up in Settings, in the order Settings lists them, such as Claude Sonnet or Gemini Pro. That takes a few seconds to half a minute and costs about a cent. A run writes with the cheap model of the run's own cloud provider, else of the first one set up, about a tenth of a cent a picture on Gemini Flash.

Your note on the book's world

The picture sheet has a line for your own note on the book's world, such as "Gaslit London in the 1890s". It is empty until you write one: choose Add a note about this book's world. There is one note per book edition, used in every style, and the model reads it with every picture of the book. A note changes the prompts written after it, so choose Write it again to use it in the prompt on screen. Lorekeeper no longer writes a world note of its own.

Keeping one face

Each picture is drawn on its own, so a face is kept by sending the image model a portrait to follow. Lorekeeper decides which portrait belongs to which figure by the character's entry in the index, not by a name, so a scene shows the right face whatever the character is called in the prompt.

Labels in a scene

Before the model writes a scene, Lorekeeper checks which of the people and creatures it may show have a portrait you could see at that point. At most four do, most connected first. Each gets a neutral label, "figure A", "figure B" and so on, on its entry. The model is told to call a labelled figure by its label the first time, followed by who they are in a few words ("figure A, a tidewarden in a dark travelling coat"), and by the label or a pronoun after that. It does not describe their face, hair, eyes, skin or build, because the portrait gives those. A figure it does not draw, such as someone heard, unseen or out of frame, is never labelled. Someone with no portrait is described as before. The prompt still never holds a name.

The portraits are attached by what the text says, not by what the model listed. A portrait goes with the picture only for a label that appears in the prompt, in label order. The model is checked: a label it was never given is written once more, and then dropped.

The image provider gets a short note beside the prompt, not inside it: "Reference picture 1 is figure A. Reference picture 2 is figure B. Draw each labelled figure with the face, hair, colouring and build of their reference picture." When a reference is in another style, the note adds that only the likeness is to be taken, never the drawing style, numbered the same way. The note is never part of the prompt you see or edit.

What an edit does

The labels are plain words in the prompt box, and what you leave in it is what is drawn. Take "figure B" out and the portrait for B is not sent and the note does not mention it. Type a label the sheet did not give, or one whose portrait is missing, and nothing is attached for it. If you rewrite a refused prompt with Rewrite it so it can be drawn, the new text keeps the labels of the figures it still draws and cannot add one. Same prompt in Redraw keeps the labels and their references and rebuilds the note from the labels still in the text. Drawing the scene in another style takes each label's portrait from that style's set, or a likeness from another style when it has none.

One face over a book

A portrait drawn later in the book is sent the same character's earlier portrait as a reference, whether or not their look changed. The note says so: "Reference picture 1 is this same figure earlier in the story: keep their face, hair, colouring and build, except where the prompt describes a change." So a character does not turn blonde because the later notes stopped mentioning black hair. The earlier looks in the prompt keep the words, and the earlier portrait keeps the picture. A first portrait has no reference. The earlier portrait must be one you could see at that point, drawn by version 0.18.0 or later, and from a book you have finished.

Another style

The portrait can come from another style. When the style you are drawing in has no portrait of a character yet, the latest portrait of them in another style of the same book is sent instead. A comic-strip scene can then show the face of a digital-art portrait. The image model is told, beside the reference and not in your prompt, to take only the likeness from it: face, hair, build and marks, never the drawing style. Lorekeeper reads the other style's pictures and never changes them. The picture's record names the style each such reference came from.

In the picture sheet, under the prompt, a line says which figure is which: "Figure A is Orrin", with the portrait's small round thumbnail. The names come from what you have read, so someone you have not met yet shows as "Figure A" with no name. The lines follow the prompt box as you type. In the lightbox, How this was drawn lists the same lines for a drawn picture. In the sheet, the thumbnail is the reference the server attached when it wrote the prompt, and the name follows your position. In the lightbox, a thumbnail shows only while that portrait is itself one you could see, so a reference in another style shows the name without a picture, and both the name and the thumbnail follow your position as you move.

This gives a character you can recognise from picture to picture. It does not give the same face every time. A new pose or new light can still drift, and in a crowded scene the image model may follow a reference loosely or not at all.

Pictures drawn by an earlier version

Pictures drawn before version 0.18.0 stay as they are, and so do their prompts. Nothing is redrawn on its own. When you redraw one, Write a new prompt is the first choice, so it gets a prompt written the new way. To see which version your server runs, look at the foot of the section list in Settings, or point at the Lorekeeper name at the top of the rail. Earlier versions wrote notes on each character's look and an automatic world note. Those notes are kept in the set but no longer read, and a picture drawn from them is not sent as a reference for a new one. A world note you wrote yourself becomes your note on the book's world.

In a series

A later book builds on the earlier books' pictures. A character who already has a portrait is not drawn again unless the book records a change of look. Who counts as a main character is decided for each book on its own events, so a character who matters in book one but has a small part in book two gets no new portrait in book two. Scenes in book five use the portrait from book one, or its latest version. A prompt can use what the earlier books' index says about a character, and your note on an earlier book's world when this book has none.

What you see still follows what you have finished. A picture from an earlier book shows once you mark that book finished. Text from a book you have not finished stays hidden: your note on that book's world, and the prompt under "How this was drawn" when the prompt used that book, which instead says "How this was drawn is hidden until you finish" and the book's title. When you draw on demand, the prompt is written without anything from an unfinished book: none of its entries, passages, words or pictures.

Spoilers

Every picture belongs to a point in the book, the same kind of point a name, a detail or an event has. A chapter picture belongs to the end of its chapter, a moment to the moment, and a portrait to the description it was drawn from. The Reader shows a picture only once your position reaches that point. Before then it is not on the page, not in the counts and not behind the lightbox's arrows. Moving back hides it again.

A prompt is written only from what comes at or before its point: the passages, and the index as it stands there. The book's title and author are the one exception: the model may know the book, and it is told to use nothing beyond what it was given.

The prompt is checked before it is sent. It is written once more if it names a person or creature, uses a word the book has not used yet, reads with hindsight ("would later", "turns out", "now"), talks about the text rather than the picture, or states a family or romantic tie, such as "mother", "wife" or "lover", that nothing sent with it states. If the second try fails for any reason but a name, the picture is skipped. The calls that mark which details describe a look, pick the sentences of an entry's notes that state a look, and screen a chapter's moments read further into the index, but they only choose: they return numbers, nothing they write goes into a prompt, and what they mark counts only from its own point. The sentence pick is made once for each character in each book and kept, by the cheap model. In a run it is made while the portraits are planned. The sheet makes it the first time it needs it, which is the one place a prompt waits on such a call, and falls back to the older descriptions if it fails. A picture is less exact than text, which helps, but a model that knows the book could still put something later into a scene without naming anyone, and no check catches that.

What is sent where

  • The text model writes the prompt. It gets what How a prompt is written lists: the index text visible at the picture's point, up to 900 words of the book's paragraphs, never past that point, the book's title, author and series, and your note on the book's world. If the book was built on the same cloud provider, it already read that text then.
  • The image provider gets only the written prompt and the reference portraits, with a short note saying which reference is which figure and, when a reference is in another style, that only its likeness is wanted. It never gets the book's text or the EPUB.

Pictures stay on your server. They are not part of share bundles or the community registry.

GPT Image 2 costs a little more for a square picture than for a portrait or a landscape. OpenAI bills GPT Image 2.6 by tokens and publishes no price per picture, so Lorekeeper shows "price unknown" before you draw. It works out the cost of each picture afterwards from the tokens OpenAI reports.

For a whole book: Dracula at the main level, with portraits of the main characters, is 28 chapter pictures and 23 portraits of 10 characters, 51 pictures in all, and comes to about $1.71 on Nano Banana 2.1 at standard quality (51 times $0.0336). Portraits of everyone with a part make it 48 portraits and 76 pictures, about $2.55. Every moment worth drawing can cost several times that. Writing the prompts adds about a tenth of a cent per picture on Gemini Flash, measured on four books, and a run spends a little more choosing the moments and planning the portraits. In a series, a later book draws only the new faces and the changed ones.

Every run has a cap. The build form sets it to $10. In the form and in Settings it can be set from $1 to $500. lorekeeper illustrate --cap takes any amount above $0. A run that reaches the cap stops drawing and keeps what it drew. The cap counts the pictures, the prompts and any call that may have been billed though no picture came back. Drawing one picture on demand has no cap: the sheet shows the price before you choose Draw. Its prompt is written with a strong model and costs about a cent.

When a provider refuses

Image models refuse some scenes, mostly violence, gore and horror. The staking in Dracula and much of a war novel are likely to be refused. During a run, a refused picture is written once more as a quieter composition. If that is refused too, the picture is skipped and the report gives the provider's reason. A skipped picture is not tried again on resume. A skipped chapter picture leaves that chapter with no picture rather than a wrong one, though at "Every moment worth drawing" its other moments can still have theirs.

A picture you draw from the Reader is drawn from the prompt the sheet shows, and it is never rewritten without you. If it is refused, the sheet says so in one sentence with the provider's reason and keeps your prompt so you can change it. Under the reason, Rewrite it so it can be drawn asks the text model for a quieter picture of the same moment. It keeps who is there, where, the light and the mood. It shows what is hard to look at by implication: a figure covered, a face turned away, the moment before or after, the faces of those watching. It leaves out graphic detail, wounds and bodies shown dead, and it gives no age for anyone young. It never tries to trick the filter with misspellings, code words or another language. The new text goes into the prompt box with your style kept, and nothing is drawn until you read it and choose Draw. If that is refused too, the button is offered again. The rewrite reads the same material as the prompt, so it costs about a cent.

Drawing a whole book

There are two ways to draw a whole book.

  • When you build. Open Pictures on the build form, the section after Options, and tick Draw pictures. Choose the style, "The main moment of each chapter" or "Every moment worth drawing", the portraits ("Main characters", "Everyone with a part" or "No portraits"), the quality, the image provider when you have more than one, and where to stop. The form shows an estimate such as "About 52 pictures, about $1.75". Pictures are drawn after the index is written, as the last stage of the run.
  • For a book that already has an index. The book page has Add pictures, or More pictures once it has some. It opens the same choices and starts a run that draws without building the index again.

The Runs page shows "Drawing pictures" as the run's last phase, with a line such as "Pictures 12 of 52, $0.40 on pictures". A run can be stopped and resumed. A resume draws the rest and never draws a picture twice. If the drawing fails, the index stays as the run wrote it, with every picture drawn so far.

Drawing one picture

In the Reader, Draw a picture is a quiet button under a chapter's recap, at the end of a Timeline row (shown when you point at the row, or tap it on a touch screen), and under an entry's name. Any person, creature, group, place or thing can be drawn this way. In a series, the entry button is offered only for an entry that has appeared in the book you are reading, and it is not shown in edit mode. The button appears when an image provider is set up or the book already has pictures, and only when the server has the book file.

It opens a sheet. The sheet writes a prompt from what you have read, which takes a few seconds to half a minute and costs about a cent. For a chapter, it lists the moments you can draw. You can then:

  • change the prompt, or choose Write it again. Under the prompt, a line for each figure label tells you who "figure A" is; keep the label to keep that portrait as a reference;
  • pick a style, the shape, the quality, the provider and the model, all at once with no new prompt;
  • add or change your note on the book's world;
  • see the price, then choose Draw.

Drawing the same moment again in another style uses the same prompt, with no wait. Drawing an entry that already has a picture adds a new picture beside the old one. To swap a picture for a new one, use Redraw instead.

The picture is filed where it belongs in the book, not at your position, so it shows at the same place for every reader of that index.

Redraw, delete and undo

Click a picture to open it full size. The arrows step through the other pictures of that chapter or entry that you can see. How this was drawn shows the prompt, which portrait was used for each figure, the style, the model, the quality, the cost and the date. Delete asks first.

Redraw offers two choices:

  • Write a new prompt closes the picture and opens the picture sheet, titled "Redraw", for the same chapter, moment or entry. The prompt is written from what you have read so far, as for a new picture, and you can change it, the style and the rest before you choose Draw.
  • Same prompt draws the saved prompt again with the same references and figure labels, with an optional note put first, such as "in daylight, closer to the face".

For a picture drawn before version 0.18.0, Write a new prompt comes first, since its prompt was written the older way. For a newer picture, Same prompt comes first. Either way the new picture takes the old one's place. If you change the style on the sheet, the new picture goes into that style's set instead and the old one stays where it is. Write a new prompt is offered only when an image provider is set up and you can see what the picture shows at your position. Otherwise Redraw goes straight to the same prompt.

Each change can be undone from the message that confirms it. Undo uses the same history as hand edits and duplicate merges, and it reverts only the pictures that change touched, so pictures drawn since are kept.

Showing and hiding pictures

When a book has pictures, the Reader's head row has a Pictures switch, and a style menu when the book has been drawn in more than one style. On a phone both are in the ⋯ menu. Your choices are remembered in this browser. With pictures off, no picture shows, and the Draw a picture buttons stay. A book with no pictures and no image provider looks as it always did.

Settings

Under Settings, Cloud models has an Images column. It names each provider's image model and its price for one picture, or says "Can't draw".

Pictures holds the defaults that new pictures start from: the style, the quality, where a run stops, and the image provider. They are kept in this browser. Below them is a list of the pictures on this server, by book, with their styles and size, and Remove pictures, which asks first and can be undone. A book cannot lose its pictures while a run is drawing for it.

From the command line

lorekeeper illustrate draws a set of pictures for an index into art/<textHash>/<style>/ beside it. It never writes the index, and a second run resumes where the first stopped. Pass the EPUB the index was built from:

uv run --extra claude lorekeeper illustrate examples/dracula/dracula.json \
  --epub corpus/pg345/pg345.epub --style ink --level main --provider gemini --quality draft --cap 3

--level all draws every moment worth drawing, --portraits all draws everyone with a part instead of the main characters, --portraits none (or --no-portraits) leaves out the portraits, --chapters 4-6 draws chapter pictures only for those table of contents entries (see lorekeeper toc) and portraits only up to the end of the last of them, including people met earlier, and --limit N draws at most N pictures. The image key comes from GEMINI_API_KEY or OPENAI_API_KEY, or from --api-key-file. The prompts are written by Claude's cheap model when ANTHROPIC_API_KEY is set, else by the image provider's cheap text model; --text-provider and --text-model choose another. For a series, pass the books in reading order with one --epub each and --series, or pass earlier books with --prior to read their pictures and entries without drawing them.

lorekeeper draw draws one picture from a prompt you give, which is a quick way to try a provider or a style:

uv run lorekeeper draw "A ruined castle on a crag at dusk, one lit window." -o castle --provider gemini --shape landscape

It writes the file with the extension of the format the provider sent and prints the model, size and cost. --ref FILE adds a picture to follow, up to six.

For developers

Pictures are not part of the index format or the /v0 protocol, and the specification does not cover them. In this version they belong to the reference implementation: the web app, lorekeeper illustrate and lorekeeper draw. Each set is a folder per book edition and style, with an art.json manifest that lists each picture with its kind, what it shows, its from anchor, the prompt as sent, the provider, the model and the cost. A scene also has figures, [{"label", "entity", "ref"}]: each label in its prompt, the entity it stands for and the reference sent for it (or none). Its references are picture ids of the same set, or {"set": slug, "id": id} for a likeness taken from another style. A picture drawn with replaces took the place of the picture with that id. The Reader compares a picture's from anchor with the reader's position exactly as it compares any other item's.
