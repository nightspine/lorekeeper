Pictures

Lorekeeper can draw pictures for a book: one for each chapter, more for the moments worth seeing, and a portrait of each main character. The Reader shows a picture only once you have read up to the point it belongs to.

Pictures are off until you ask for them. They need an image provider, which is a Gemini or OpenAI key, and a cloud text model to write the prompts. Claude cannot draw, but it can write the prompts while Gemini or OpenAI draws. Pictures are kept beside the index, never inside it, so an index you share or install stays text only.

What can be drawn

  • Chapter pictures. One picture for the main moment of a chapter. It sits under the chapter's title on the Chapters tab.
  • Moments. A picture for another moment of a chapter, shown at the left of its event on the Timeline.
  • Portraits. One for each of the book's main characters: the people and creatures the story is most about, up to 12 a book. A character counts as main when it is named in at least one in twenty of the book's events, and in at least 3. Small moments do not count. When more than 12 qualify, the 12 named most are drawn. "Everyone with a part" draws a wider cast: every person and creature with a description who is named in two of the book's events, small moments not counted, or who comes up often enough to get a description written from every passage about them. In Dracula that is 32 of the 51 characters with a description. The portrait sits beside "Who they are" in the entry, and a small round one replaces the type mark in the Cast list. When the character's look changes for good, a new portrait is drawn and the old one moves to "Earlier impressions". A lasting wound or scar, a lost eye, a disguise, a body remade or a new way of dressing all count. A death alone does not. A character has at most three portraits per book. You can still draw a portrait of anyone else from their entry in the Reader.
  • Places and things. These are never drawn ahead of time, but you can draw any of them on demand from its entry.

A character the text never describes gets no portrait. The run's report says "skipped: no look described" and does not try again until the book describes them.

To find where a character is described, a cheap text call reads every detail and description the index holds about them in the book. For each one it says whether it tells how they look, and whether it records a lasting change of their look. It only marks items. Nothing it writes goes into a prompt, and an item counts only from its own point in the book. A first portrait is drawn at the first item that describes the look, and a new version at each change, at most three a book. The call works the same way for any book, since it reads the index's own words rather than a list of look words.

How many pictures a chapter gets

Before drawing a chapter, a cheap text call reads the chapter's events and picks the ones that can be drawn: a moment with a place, people or things in view. "Harker finds every door locked" can be drawn. "Harker concludes he is a prisoner" cannot. Small moments are never drawn. That count is the most pictures the chapter can have.

When you build, you choose one of two levels:

  • The main moment of each chapter draws one picture per chapter. It is the chapter's turning point when one can be drawn, else the moment the chapter recap says most about, else the recap itself.
  • Every moment worth drawing draws all the moments the check picked.

A chapter with no events gets no picture. In the Reader, the picture sheet for a chapter says how many moments it has so far and how many are drawn, for example "4 moments in this chapter so far, 1 drawn", and you can draw the rest one at a time.

Styles

Nine styles come with Lorekeeper: Digital art, Comic strip, Anime, Realism, Hand-drawn, Oil painting, Epic sci-fi, Watercolour and Ink. Each is one sentence added to every prompt, such as "Black ink drawing, fine cross-hatching, strong contrast, aged paper." for Ink. Choose Custom to write your own sentence, for example "linocut, two colours". Describe the look, never an artist's name.

Each style is a separate set of pictures. You can draw a book in Ink and again in Watercolour, and the Reader's style menu switches between them.

Keeping one world and one face

Each picture is drawn on its own, so the prompts carry what must stay the same. The text model writes the scene, and Lorekeeper then adds these notes word for word, so every prompt that uses one gets the same words.

  • The era note. The time of the book, its technology and what things are made of, true of every picture: horse-drawn carriages, oil lamps and stone, or steel corridors and screens. It says nothing about the look of any one place or people, since the book shows many. It is written once from the first chapters of the story, until they add up to at least 1,500 words. That is why a fortress in a science-fiction novel stays a science-fiction fortress when the passage only says "the fortress". The picture sheet shows it as "World". For the book's own note it has a Change button, and a note you changed is never rewritten. A note inherited from an earlier book in a series shows once you have finished that book, and it cannot be changed from the sheet.
  • The appearance note. How a character, a group, a place or a thing looks, for example "A Transylvanian nobleman: tall, thin, pale complexion, long white moustache". It starts with who they are, then lists the details of their look. It is written from the parts of the index that describe their look and the paragraphs those parts cite, then from other paragraphs that mention them. Every detail must be quoted from that text, word for word, or it is left out. A character's mood, situation or character is not a look and stays out. When the book later describes more of the look, the note is filled in: new details are added and none are dropped. When the look changes, the note is amended. A detail the change makes untrue can be replaced, but only by one quoted from the change itself, so rust-red hair becomes golden hair only where the book says so.
  • The look of their people. A character can belong to groups: a family, a house, a caste, an order, a crew. A group's note says what its members share in how they look and dress. A character's note carries the look of up to three of their groups, the smallest group first, without naming any of them, for example "Their people: golden eyes and hair". A group is added only when its look says something the character's own note does not. The character's own look comes first and wins where the two disagree. When a character's own look changes after they join a group, that group's look is no longer added: someone remade to look like another people is drawn as they now look. Which groups a character belongs to is chosen by a cheap text call from the index's own entries, and a group counts only from the point where the index first ties the character to it.
  • Where they belong. A portrait is drawn in the surroundings the character belongs to at that point in the book, not in the book's opening. That is the place their events are most often set in, the latest chapters counting most, with the place's own appearance note when it has one. Without that, it is where the members of their groups live, from the group's note. Without either, the background is plain. A scene is set where its moment happens, with that place's note when it has one. So a miner of the tunnels and a lord of the high city get different backgrounds, though the book has one era note. Indexes built before events recorded where they happen have no settings, so their portraits take the groups' homes or a plain background. Building the index again gives each character their own places.
  • The portrait as a reference. When a scene shows a character who has a portrait, that portrait is sent with the prompt as a picture to follow. A scene sends at most four, for the characters it shows, most connected first. A new portrait after a change of look is drawn with the earlier one as a reference, so the same face ages rather than a new one appearing.

This gives a character you can recognise from picture to picture. It does not give the same face every time. A new pose or new light can still drift, and in a crowded scene the image model may follow a reference loosely or not at all.

The prompt never names anyone. It describes people by their look and role, since a name invites the image model to draw what it remembers of the book. A prompt with a name in it is written once more. If the second prompt still names someone, the name is replaced by a role and the prompt is checked again. The picture is skipped if it still fails.

Pictures drawn before these notes existed stay as they are, and so do their prompts. Nothing is redrawn. A world note written by an earlier version described the opening's places and clothing as true of every picture. It is set aside and an era note is written in its place, unless you changed the note yourself. A new picture of the same character gets a new, fuller note, and an older picture is not sent as a reference for it.

In a series

A later book builds on the earlier books' pictures. It starts from their era note and appearance notes, and a character who already has a portrait is not drawn again unless the book records a change of look. Who counts as a main character is decided for each book on its own events, so a character who matters in book one but has a small part in book two gets no new portrait in book two. Scenes in book five use the portrait from book one, or its latest version. A later book may add one sentence to the era note when its own opening shows a change of era or technology. Pictures before that sentence's point use the older note.

What you see still follows what you have finished. A picture from an earlier book shows once you mark that book finished. Text from a book you have not finished stays hidden: the era note's part from that book, its characters' and groups' notes, and the prompt under "How this was drawn" when the prompt used that book, which instead says "How this was drawn is hidden until you finish" and the book's title. When you draw on demand, the prompt is written without anything from an unfinished book.

Spoilers

Every picture belongs to a point in the book, the same kind of point a name, a detail or an event has. A chapter picture belongs to the end of its chapter, a moment to the moment, and a portrait to the description it was drawn from. The Reader shows a picture only once your position reaches that point. Before then it is not on the page, not in the counts and not behind the lightbox's arrows. Moving back hides it again.

A prompt is written only from what comes at or before its point: the passage, and the index as it stands there. Pictures drawn early on wait a little longer. Because the era note is written from the opening chapters, a picture drawn from those chapters, usually a portrait, shows from the end of that opening stretch.

The prompt is checked before it is sent. It is written once more if it uses a name the book has not used yet, reads with hindsight ("would later", "turns out"), or states a family or romantic tie, such as "mother", "wife" or "lover", that nothing sent with the prompt states: the passage, the era note, the appearance notes and the index text. A second cheap call reads the written prompt and lists any phrase that gives a figure a look the notes and passage do not state. Such a prompt is written once more, and if the look is still there those words are taken out. If the second try fails for another reason, the picture is skipped. The calls that mark which details describe a look, pick a character's groups and screen a chapter's moments read further into the index, but they only choose: nothing they write goes into a prompt, and what they mark counts only from its own point. A picture is less exact than text, which helps, but a model that knows the book could still put something later into a scene without naming anyone, and no check catches that.

What is sent where

  • The text model writes the prompt. It gets the passage the picture shows, at most 600 words and never past the picture's point, and the index text visible there: the event, the chapter so far, the profiles and details of the people in it, the era note, their appearance notes and their groups' notes. To write a note it gets the details the index holds on that character, group or place up to that point and the paragraphs that mention them. If the book was built on the same cloud provider, it already read that text then. It is the cheap model of the run's own cloud provider, else of the first one set up in Settings.
  • The image provider gets only the written prompt and the reference portraits. It never gets the book's text or the EPUB.

Pictures stay on your server. They are not part of share bundles or the community registry.

GPT Image 2 costs a little more for a square picture than for a portrait or a landscape. OpenAI bills GPT Image 2.6 by tokens and publishes no price per picture, so Lorekeeper shows "price unknown" before you draw. It works out the cost of each picture afterwards from the tokens OpenAI reports.

For a whole book: Dracula at the main level, with portraits of the main characters, is 28 chapter pictures and 23 portraits of 10 characters, 51 pictures in all, and comes to about $1.71 on Nano Banana 2.1 at standard quality (51 times $0.0336). Portraits of everyone with a part make it 48 portraits and 76 pictures, about $2.55. Every moment worth drawing can cost several times that. Writing the prompts and notes adds a fraction of a cent per picture on a cheap model: about 10 to 20 cents for a whole novel on Gemini 2.5 Flash, measured on four books, and about three to four times that on Claude Haiku. In a series, a later book draws only the new faces and the changed ones.

Every run has a cap. The build form sets it to $10. In the form and in Settings it can be set from $1 to $500. lorekeeper illustrate --cap takes any amount above $0. A run that reaches the cap stops drawing and keeps what it drew. The cap counts the pictures, the prompts and any call that may have been billed though no picture came back. Drawing one picture on demand has no cap: the sheet shows the price before you choose Draw.

When a provider refuses

Image models refuse some scenes, mostly violence, gore and horror. The staking in Dracula and much of a war novel are likely to be refused. During a run, a refused picture is written once more as a quieter composition. If that is refused too, the picture is skipped and the report gives the provider's reason. A skipped picture is not tried again on resume. A skipped chapter picture leaves that chapter with no picture rather than a wrong one, though at "Every moment worth drawing" its other moments can still have theirs.

A picture you draw from the Reader is drawn from the prompt the sheet shows, and that prompt is never rewritten. If it is refused, the sheet says so in one sentence with the provider's reason and keeps your prompt so you can change it.

Drawing a whole book

There are two ways to draw a whole book.

  • When you build. Open Pictures on the build form, the section after Options, and tick Draw pictures. Choose the style, "The main moment of each chapter" or "Every moment worth drawing", the portraits ("Main characters", "Everyone with a part" or "No portraits"), the quality, the image provider when you have more than one, and where to stop. The form shows an estimate such as "About 52 pictures, about $1.75". Pictures are drawn after the index is written, as the last stage of the run.
  • For a book that already has an index. The book page has Add pictures, or More pictures once it has some. It opens the same choices and starts a run that draws without building the index again.

The Runs page shows "Drawing pictures" as the run's last phase, with a line such as "Pictures 12 of 52, $0.40 on pictures". A run can be stopped and resumed. A resume draws the rest and never draws a picture twice. If the drawing fails, the index stays as the run wrote it, with every picture drawn so far.

Drawing one picture

In the Reader, Draw a picture is a quiet button under a chapter's recap, at the end of a Timeline row (shown when you point at the row, or tap it on a touch screen), and under an entry's name when it has no picture yet. Any person, creature, group, place or thing can be drawn this way. In a series, the entry button is offered only for an entry that has appeared in the book you are reading, and it is not shown in edit mode. The button appears when an image provider is set up or the book already has pictures, and only when the server has the book file.

It opens a sheet. The sheet writes a prompt from what you have read, which takes a few seconds and costs a fraction of a cent. For a chapter, it lists the moments you can draw. You can then:

  • change the prompt, or choose Write it again;
  • pick a style, which writes the prompt again since it carries the style (it asks first when you changed the prompt);
  • pick the shape and the quality;
  • read the era note, and change it when it is this book's own;
  • see the price, then choose Draw.

The picture is filed where it belongs in the book, not at your position, so it shows at the same place for every reader of that index.

Redraw, delete and undo

Click a picture to open it full size. The arrows step through the other pictures of that chapter or entry that you can see. How this was drawn shows the prompt, the style, the model, the quality, the cost and the date. Redraw draws it again from the same prompt and references, with an optional note put first, such as "in daylight, closer to the face". The new picture replaces the old one. Delete asks first.

Each change can be undone from the message that confirms it. Undo uses the same history as hand edits and duplicate merges, and it reverts only the pictures that change touched, so pictures drawn since are kept.

Showing and hiding pictures

When a book has pictures, the Reader's head row has a Pictures switch, and a style menu when the book has been drawn in more than one style. On a phone both are in the ⋯ menu. Your choices are remembered in this browser. With pictures off, no picture shows, and the Draw a picture buttons stay. A book with no pictures and no image provider looks as it always did.

Settings

Under Settings, Cloud models has an Images column. It names each provider's image model and its price for one picture, or says "Can't draw".

Pictures holds the defaults that new pictures start from: the style, the quality, where a run stops, and the image provider. They are kept in this browser. Below them is a list of the pictures on this server, by book, with their styles and size, and Remove pictures, which asks first and can be undone. A book cannot lose its pictures while a run is drawing for it.

From the command line

lorekeeper illustrate draws a set of pictures for an index into art/<textHash>/<style>/ beside it. It never writes the index, and a second run resumes where the first stopped. Pass the EPUB the index was built from:

uv run --extra claude lorekeeper illustrate examples/dracula/dracula.json \
  --epub corpus/pg345/pg345.epub --style ink --level main --provider gemini --quality draft --cap 3

--level all draws every moment worth drawing, --portraits all draws everyone with a part instead of the main characters, --portraits none (or --no-portraits) leaves out the portraits, --chapters 4-6 draws chapter pictures only for those table of contents entries (see lorekeeper toc) and portraits only up to the end of the last of them, including people met earlier, and --limit N draws at most N pictures. The image key comes from GEMINI_API_KEY or OPENAI_API_KEY, or from --api-key-file. The prompts are written by Claude's cheap model when ANTHROPIC_API_KEY is set, else by the image provider's cheap text model; --text-provider and --text-model choose another. For a series, pass the books in reading order with one --epub each and --series, or pass earlier books with --prior to read their pictures and notes without drawing them.

lorekeeper draw draws one picture from a prompt you give, which is a quick way to try a provider or a style:

uv run lorekeeper draw "A ruined castle on a crag at dusk, one lit window." -o castle --provider gemini --shape landscape

It writes the file with the extension of the format the provider sent and prints the model, size and cost. --ref FILE adds a picture to follow, up to six.

For developers

Pictures are not part of the index format or the /v0 protocol, and the specification does not cover them. In this version they belong to the reference implementation: the web app, lorekeeper illustrate and lorekeeper draw. Each set is a folder per book edition and style, with an art.json manifest that lists each picture with its kind, what it shows, its from anchor, the prompt as sent, the provider, the model and the cost. The Reader compares a picture's from anchor with the reader's position exactly as it compares any other item's.
